Where the time actually goes

This is the finding the whole plan is built on, and it is not where anyone expects it to be.

More than half the labour in a custom cabinet business happens before a single sheet is cut. That is why custom cabinets cost three times stock: you are not paying for better manufacturing, you are paying for a designer's afternoon.

Which means the bottleneck is a person at a desk

ConstraintKitchens per year
Machines — CNC and edgebander228
Shop floor — 2 people306
Front office — 1 designer138Binds

The machines idle at 60% while the designer is saturated. Buying robots for the shop floor would change nothing at all — you would be automating the station that already has 40% spare capacity.

What AI is worth here

Take design and engineering from 13.5 hours to 1.5 — AI drafts, a human approves — and the constraint moves to the machines at 228 kitchens a year. Revenue goes from $2.42m to $4.00m and EBITDA from $1.22m to $2.28m, with the same building, the same machines and the same three people. That is a $1.06m/year return on software.

The AI stack

Six layers. Three are genuinely AI, three are deterministic code — and knowing which is which is the whole architecture.

A router costs $150,000 and anyone can buy one. The app that takes somebody standing in their own kitchen holding a laser and returns a priced, engineered, machine-ready job is what lets us quote a price a shop routing the work through a designer cannot match.

01

Intake and measurement

AI + human check

The customer measures their own kitchen with a shipped laser distance meter, guided step by step by the app: three heights per wall, both diagonals, floor level at five points, services and appliances. A phone scan and photos ride alongside as corroboration, not as the measurement. The app cross-checks every reading and returns a dimensioned room model with a confidence score on each span.

Replaces the site visit outright, at roughly $140 a kitchen saved.

02

Layout generation

AI

A constraint solver generates valid layouts — appliance clearances, the 15" landing space beside a range, filler strips, standard box widths, door swing, code. A language model handles the fuzzy half: turning "we bake a lot" and "a coffee station near the window" into storage allocation and placement. Three to five options, rendered.

Replaces 4–6 hours of designer time. The customer picks.

03

Quoting

Deterministic

Layout → box schedule → parts → materials → price. Pure arithmetic over the parametric model. This binds you to a contract, so it must be exactly reproducible and never generative.

Instant, versus days. This alone changes how the business sells.

04

Engineering and CAM

Deterministic

Box schedule → part list → nesting solver → G-code, edgebander program, hardware pick list, part labels, assembly sheets. Cabinet Vision, Microvellum and Mozaik already do exactly this; it is solved software, not an AI problem.

Replaces 4.5 hours of engineering. Simulated before it runs.

05

Scheduling and shop orchestration

AI

Which jobs batch onto which sheets, in what order, to minimise offcut waste and still hit promise dates. Re-nesting when a part fails QC. Reordering the queue when material is late. This is a genuine optimisation problem and it is where AI belongs on the shop floor.

Sheet yield is the second-largest variable cost after doors.

06

Customer and supplier agents

AI

Order status, change requests, install scheduling, purchase orders to the door supplier and the sheet distributor, chasing late material.

Replaces most of an operations coordinator.

06

Automating the front office

The engineering plan for the 13.5 hours. This is the whole company, so it is worth being specific about how it gets built and how we know it works.

One object, and everything else is a view of it

The architectural decision that makes this tractable: there is exactly one model of a kitchen, and the quote, the cut list, the G‑code, the labels and the hardware schedule are all derived from it. The three problems that are actually hard

01

A layout a human would not redraw

The real AI problem

Clearances, the 15″ landing beside a range, work triangle, standard box widths, door swing, code — those are a constraint solver, and solvers are reliable. The hard half is taste and intent: turning “we bake a lot” and “coffee near the window” into storage allocation. A language model proposes; the solver filters to legal; the customer chooses. The measurable target is the share of generated layouts a designer accepts with no edit, tracked from day one.

02

Tolerance propagation

Deterministic, and unforgiving

Measurement uncertainty has to flow through to a decision: this run is inside its envelope and can cut, or it needs wider fillers, or it needs a human. Every run carries its own envelope derived from the wall's plumb and square deviation. Getting this wrong scraps a $17,550 kitchen, which is why the verdict is arithmetic and never a model output.

03

The post-processor

Deterministic, simulated, reviewed line by line

The one component where a wrong answer is a machine crash rather than a wasted sheet. Generated by agents like everything else, but reviewed by a person, versioned, and simulated before every single run. A language model never emits machine code — a hallucinated depth or feed rate on a 24,000 rpm spindle is a crash, not a typo.

07

The manufacturing plan

Sheet in, finished cabinet out, nobody touching it. Eight stations, two robots and a case clamp — and one architectural fact that makes the whole thing tractable.

The fact the plan rests on

A cabinet carcass is topologically identical every time

Five or six panels, the same relationships, the same assembly order. Between a 300mm wall unit and a 1200mm base unit only the numbers change. That is not true of a chair, a table or almost any other furniture, and it is why robotic assembly is a reasonable bet here and a bad one elsewhere.

It means the robot is never taught a cabinet. The same parametric model that emits the cut list and the G‑code emits the assembly sequence — panel order, insertion vectors, clamp positions. Batch size one costs nothing, because the program is generated per box rather than selected from a library.

The case clamp is not the constraint — feeding it is

A through-feed case clamp closes and squares one cabinet a minute. At 14 boxes a kitchen that is 53 machine-hours a year at 228 kitchens, and 142 at 610. The clamp will never be busy. All the engineering is in getting the right five panels to it, the right way up, with the connectors already in — which is a part-handling and sequencing problem, not an assembly problem.

That is the distinction worth holding onto. We are not building a robot that understands cabinets. We are building a robot that places panels a parametric model has already told it exactly where to put.

The one decision this forces

Assembling in the building means shipping assembled boxes, and an assembled carcass is roughly four times the volume of its parts. Flat-pack — shipping panels and having the installer snap locking joints together on site — is the alternative. Both are viable and they trade against each other.
