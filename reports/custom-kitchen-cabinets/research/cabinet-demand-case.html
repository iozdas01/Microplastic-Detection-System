The three numbers

TAM

$16.02bn — the size of the pond

What all 6,118 American cabinet makers sell between them, at manufacturer prices. Not our revenue. It is the number that says this is a real industry rather than a niche.

SAM

$182.9m — the part we could compete for

Custom cabinets only (7.6% of the industry), and only the share that can be sold direct rather than through a showroom. Still not our revenue — it is what is available to win.

SOM

$4.0m — what we could actually build

One panel cell running two shifts makes 3,192 cabinet boxes a year, or 228 kitchens. That is 2% of the SAM — so the factory runs out long before the market does, and no guess about market share is needed.

This is why the SOM is a capacity number and not a percentage. Assuming "we will take 2% of the market" would be inventing the most important figure in the model. Counting how many boxes a machine can cut in a year is not an assumption — it is arithmetic on a cycle time. The honest ceiling is $4.0m, and reaching it is a selling problem, not a market-size one.

Three reasons, in order of weight.

The market is five times bigger. Cabinet manufacturing is $16.02bn against $3.20bn for the category containing every chair, table and dresser.

It is the only category we can actually measure. Of the categories tested in Google Trends, 15 sit at the index floor — their search index is zero or one in more than 80% of weeks, which cannot be read. "custom cabinets" never does.

Every kitchen is a different shape. That is the point. A chair is one design made many times, which suits a mould and a brand. A kitchen is bespoke every single time, which is exactly the job automation is good at and a hand shop is slow at.

The deciding line is the last row of the table. An automated factory's advantage is cost and fit at volume; a luxury chair's advantage is brand at low volume. A chair business would not need this factory at all.

What is assumed, and which way it bends

One number in the funnel is a judgement: how much of a showroom-sold category can be won online. It is set at 15% and nothing in any public dataset measures it. A configurator with real prices would.

Three further caveats sit on the measured numbers, and they do not all point the same way. The $16.02bn includes countertops as well as cabinets, which overstates it. It counts only businesses with payroll, missing the long tail of one-person shops — 54% of establishments already have fewer than five staff. And custom cabinetry is also built by architectural woodwork firms in a different industry code worth $8.46bn, none of which is counted here. Two of the three push the market up.

The 7.6% custom share is a share of searches, not of revenue, and it excludes semi-custom entirely — a factory box with configurable sizes and finishes, which is what a configurator actually sells. So it is a floor, not a centre.
