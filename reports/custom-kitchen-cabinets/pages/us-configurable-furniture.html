The three numbers

The TAM is built from the ground up: every one of the nine article lines under the Consumer Expenditure Survey's Furniture heading, each multiplied by the number of consumer units in each income quintile. It reconciles to 66.3% of Census retail sales for NAICS 442 — the expected shape, since 442 carries home furnishings as well as furniture.

SOM is the smaller of what one cell can make (2,812 units a year, derived from measured station cycle times rather than assumed) and what the market supports (1,249). Demand binds — the cell is 2.3× larger than year-3 demand. The scarce thing here is buyers, not throughput, which is why the next dollar belongs in the configurator rather than in a faster machine.

The finding

Only one number in the SAM is measured, and it is the small one. Google Trends can see how many people search for a custom version of an article rather than a plain one: for the beachhead article that is 0.55% of the generic term. That is the demand that exists today, when "custom" means a large premium and a long wait from a joinery shop.

An autonomous factory sells configurable furniture at close to stock price. That is a different offer to a different buyer, and no available dataset measures how many people would take it. The model therefore multiplies the measured share — and that multiplier, not the market, is what the whole size of the opportunity turns on.

Measured · custom demand as it exists today $8.8m /yr

The article pool at the 0.55% search share, with no uplift at all. Defensible from the data alone.

Assumed · if removing the premium multiplies demand 6× $52.7m /yr

The base case in this report, and an assumption. At the ceiling multiple it is $131.8m.

The gap between those two bars is not a modelling detail — it is the company. The cheapest way to close it is a configurator landing page with real prices and a real checkout, measuring how many visitors configure rather than how many search. That single experiment is worth more than any further desk research on this page.

Which article to build

Which article this factory should make is an output of the model, not an input to it. Every article line is sized from household spend and scored on four dimensions: pool size (30%), custom-search share (30%), machinability (25%) and flat-packability (15%). The last two are engineering judgements about a nested-panel cell, not measurements.

Does selling in-home assembly change the answer?

A crew in the customer's home removes the reason to fear a bulky box, so the scoring was re-run with the flat-pack weight cut from 15% to 3%. It does not change the pick — Kitchen and dining room furniture wins under both weightings. The service tier changes the margin on the article, not the choice of it.

Tiers and the assembly attach

The premium price multiple is the one number in this table that is not a guess. It is the article's measured Q5 spend skew: the top income quintile already spends 2.36× what the average household does on this article, which is the observable evidence for how far it can be priced up. Tier mix, margins, attach rates and the $0 assembly price are assumptions.

There is no assembly attach. kigumi joinery assembles by hand without tools or fasteners, so white-glove assembly has nothing to do — so there is nothing to install, and nothing caps sales to the 42% of the country inside serviced metros. The attach columns are held at zero rather than deleted, so the service can be switched back on by changing numbers.

15 of 24 categories cannot be ranked at all. They sit at the index floor. Of the 9 that do resolve, 5 are built-ins — cabinets, closets, bars, vanities — rather than furniture. The best-resolved free-standing furniture is custom desk, and even that is only 48% genuinely furniture in its related searches — the rest is accessories. The honest reading is not that one article wins: it is that no free-standing customisable furniture article has enough US search demand to measure reliably, and the categories that do have volume are the ones sold through installers.

Momentum is deliberately absent. Thirteen of twenty-five series peak in the same week — 12 April 2026 — across independent query batches, which is an index artifact rather than demand; any year-on-year figure spanning it measures the artifact. The ranking uses level only, and the analysis detects that condition rather than assuming it.

How this was built

19 collectors, 14 of which ran without any credential, landing 25,708 rows. Each writes the verbatim API payload to data/raw/, a tidy table to data/processed/, and one line to data/MANIFEST.jsonl recording the URL, fetch time, row count and status. Nothing above quotes a number without a manifest line behind it.

Two substitutions were needed. USITC DataWeb requires an account token, so import figures come from UN Comtrade's public endpoint — the same US statistics, the same HS lines. The Census MRTS API is key-gated, so retail sales come from the published workbook carrying the identical series.

This page is generated by scripts/build_page_us.py from the collector outputs — every figure, table and derived claim is computed, not typed. The model's assumptions live in one block at the top of scripts/build_tam_us.py. Change an assumption there, re-run the pipeline, and this page rewrites itself.
