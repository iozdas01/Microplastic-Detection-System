A chair with no fasteners, and a factory that never assembles one

A solid-timber cell cutting kigumi joinery on 5-axis machining centres, with robots carrying parts between machines. Nothing is assembled in the building — the customer does that by hand, without tools. On land we already own, with three-phase service in place.

The proposition

Make a configurable dining chair from solid hardwood, joined the way a Japanese temple is joined: interlocking tsugite and shiguchi geometry, tapered wedges that tighten under load, and no screws, cams, dowels or glue anywhere in the product. 47 machined joint operations per chair, cut to the 0.1–0.2 mm tolerance the method needs.

The article was not chosen by preference. Every furniture line was scored on demand and on whether this process can make it, and kitchen and dining room furniture came out first — dining chairs and tables are the native home of frame joinery, and chairs sell in sets, so an order is 4 units rather than one.

Three things in this plan cut against it, and all three are load-bearing. Phase 1 costs $4.8m, well past the $1.5–3m envelope this was scoped to — solid-timber 5-axis is a more capital-hungry process than panel work, and the building adds $1.9m on top. The cell can make 2.3× what year-3 demand supports. And the plant delivers a 32% gross margin at that volume, not the 56% the pricing assumes.

Why the joinery decides the factory

Choosing kigumi is not a styling decision; it rewrites the equipment list. Three consequences follow directly:

Machines

No nesting router, no edgebander

Kigumi is cut in solid stock, not sheet. The plant is a lumber line feeding two 5-axis machining centres — the only kind of machine that can cut compound joint geometry in one setup.

Tolerance

Moisture control is a machining input

A joint held by geometry alone fails when the wood moves. Holding 0.1–0.2 mm means a kiln and a conditioning room upstream of the first cut — capex that a panel shop simply does not carry.

Product

The bill of materials has no hardware line

No cams, no dowels, no screws, no glue. It also means no assembly service to sell: the customer assembles by hand, which removes the metro coverage that would otherwise cap where we can sell at all.

The article

A dining chair in white oak, FAS, kiln dried: 9 part types, 47 joint operations, 5.67 finished board feet of timber. Every dimension in the configurator moves these numbers, and the cell does not care which variant it is cutting — that is the whole point of machining joints rather than jigging them.

There is no hardware line because kigumi has no fasteners. The timber yield does the damage instead: rough stock is 1.82x the finished volume. At $7.50 a board foot, 10.3 rough board feet is 85% of material cost. Timber yield, not hardware, is the lever on this bill of materials — every point of yield recovered by better defect-skip optimisation goes straight to margin, which is why the optimising crosscut is the first machine on the list and not an afterthought.

How it is made

Six stations, batch size one, with a 12% changeover allowance because every chair is a different size. Robots carry parts between the machining centres — that transfer is where the autonomy lives.

The slowest station sets takt at 13.4 minutes a chair, and it is the leg machining centre: the legs carry more joint operations than any other part. At 2 shifts and 72% OEE that is 11,250 chairs a year — 2,812 orders of 4, or 22 chairs a shift.

No assembly station exists. The plant machines, finishes, test-fits and flat-packs; the customer assembles by hand. That is worth stating plainly because it is what makes the automation tractable. Robotic assembly of a varying part mix is not a solved problem; robotic transfer between machines is. By moving assembly to the customer, the plant only ever has to automate the part of the job that automation is actually good at.

Should a robot assemble the chair?

Robot-assembly foundation models make this a live question rather than a settled one, so it is costed here rather than argued. Three ways to finish the job, same cell, same timber, same joints.

None of the three changes takt: a 13.4-minute cell is still gated by the leg machining centre, so an assembly station has slack to hide in. What changes is freight. An assembled chair is roughly six times the shipping volume of its parts, which takes freight from $28 to $124 a chair and packaging with it.

Shipping assembled costs $145 more a chair and takes gross margin from 32% to 9%. Almost none of that is the robot — it is the box. Robotic assembly does not fix the margin problem in this plan, it is the margin problem, because the thing that makes this product cheap to deliver is that it ships as a flat bundle of sticks.

The version worth doing

There is a robot job here, and it is the third row. Have the robot assemble every chair to prove the joints seat, then take it apart and flat-pack it. That costs $13 a chair — 2.1% of the selling price — and it attacks the one defect this product actually has. A chair held together by geometry alone either fits or it does not, and the customer is the one who finds out. A test-fit station moves that discovery inside the building.

It is also a far more tractable robotics problem. Test-fit is the same motion on a known part set in a fixture, with force feedback telling you whether a tapered joint seated. Full assembly is a varying part mix, unconstrained poses and compliance under load.

On the foundation-model route specifically

Skild AI announced S1 on 25 August 2026: a robot foundation model that learns a ten-minute task from a single human video, reporting 66% success on unseen tasks against 9% for a language-prompted policy, with kit assembly among the demonstrated tasks. The capability direction is real and it is pointed at exactly this problem.

Two things make it a phase 3 item rather than a phase 1 one. 66% first-pass success is a demo number, not a production number — a station that fails one chair in three needs a person standing next to it, which is the cost the automation was meant to remove. And at announcement there were no weights, no API and no paper, so there is nothing to integrate against or to price. The right posture is to design the test-fit cell with a swappable policy layer, run it on conventional force-controlled motion now, and adopt a foundation model when it ships with an integration path and a published success rate on a fixed part set.

16,000 sq ft on owned land. Solid timber needs more building than panel work: rough stock, the kiln and the conditioning room take floor before a part is cut. The layout reserves footprints for the phase 2 drop-ins so nothing has to move.

Where the plan does not yet hold

The cell is 2.3× bigger than the demand

Capacity is 2,812 orders a year. The market model supports 1,249 at year 3 — 6% of SAM at the blended order value — an assumption. Demand binds, and it is not the factory. The gap is narrower than it was for a panel plant, because 5-axis joinery is slow work, but it is still a plant running at 40% of what it was bought for.

The pricing assumes a margin the plant does not deliver

The tier ladder assumes 56%. At the year-3 volume of 1,124 orders — 4,496 chairs — the modelled cost is $432 a chair, or $1,727 a set, against a $2,532 order. That is 32%, not 56%. The two converge as fixed cost spreads; until they do, the cost model is the one with a factory behind it.

Both problems have one shape: this is a capital-heavy, volume-hungry plan whose demand evidence is its thinnest part. The measured custom-search share under the SAM is 0.55%; everything above it is assumption. The configurator is already a $150,000 line inside phase 1 and it is the one experiment that tests the assumption the other $4.8m rests on. Run it before the slab is poured, not after. A kigumi chair with no fasteners is also unusually cheap to prototype — one machining centre on a subcontract basis will produce a sellable chair long before there is a building to put it in.

How this was built

Demand comes from scripts/build_tam_us.py, which sizes the market from Census and BLS data and scores every furniture article on whether this process can make it. The factory comes from scripts/build_factory.py, which derives capacity from per-station cycle times and cost from a bill of materials, the BLS production wage and a named equipment schedule. Supply runs first and demand consumes its capacity and cost, so the two cannot silently disagree.

Every figure on this page is computed by scripts/build_plan_page.py from those two models — none is typed. The assumptions live in one block at the top of each script. Change one, re-run, and this page rewrites itself.
