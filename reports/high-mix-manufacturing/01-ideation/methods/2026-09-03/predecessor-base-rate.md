# Predecessor base rate — D2C made-to-order furniture

## The population rule

Anyone can re-run this. **US-headquartered · direct-to-consumer · sells physical furniture
that is made-to-order or configurable · founded 2010–2019 · raised ≥$1M institutional ·
outcome resolved by 2026-09.**

The founding-year ceiling is load-bearing: a company founded in 2022 has no outcome yet, and
including it would inflate the survival rate with companies that simply have not failed *yet*.

Deliberately excluded, with the reason, because exclusions are where a base rate gets rigged:

| Excluded | Why |
|---|---|
| Article (~$500M rev, profitable since 2015) | Canadian, and catalogue rather than made-to-order. Kept below as the category benchmark. |
| Castlery (~$180M rev, 63% growth, profitable) | Singapore. |
| Modsy ($72.7M raised, dead) | Design service, sells no furniture. |
| Havenly | Design service that became the consolidator — it is the *acquirer* in three rows below. |
| Lulu and Georgia | Decor and catalogue, not made-to-order. |
| Industry West | In distress (default judgment, pivoting to trade-only) but venture status unconfirmed. |
| Lovesac | Founded 1998, outside the window. Public and profitable. |

## The rates, at three different bars

n = 10 resolved. State the bar before quoting a number; they are not the same question.

| Bar | Definition | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| **VC bar** | Exit clearly above invested capital, or independent at scale | **1 / 10 — 10%** |
| **Survival bar** | Still independent and trading | **3 / 10 — 30%** |
| **Not-a-loss bar** | Exited above raise, or still alive and unresolved | **4 / 10 — 40%** |

Even the 10% is generous. The one exit is Joybird's $75M trade sale to a strategic — a good
outcome, and not a venture return for anyone who came in late.

With n = 10 the error band is roughly ±15 points. This distinguishes 10% from 40%. It cannot
distinguish 10% from 18%, and no amount of extra reading will fix that, because the population
itself is only this big.

## The finding — it is not the rate, it is what sorts the cohort

Sort the same ten rows by capital raised and the outcome stops looking random.

| Capital raised | Companies | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Over $20M** | Interior Define, Burrow, Floyd | **0 of 3 independent.** One dead, two rolled up |
| $1M–$10M | The Inside, Greycork, Maiden Home | 1 rolled up, 1 dead, 1 thriving |
| **Seed or below** | Inside Weather, Sabai, Maiden Home | **3 of 3 alive** |

**Every company in this cohort that raised more than $20M is gone.** Every one still
independent and growing raised single-digit millions or less. The variable that best predicts
death here is not the business model, the material, the factory, or the channel — it is how
much money the company took.

### Why that is causal rather than coincidental

The mechanism is on the record and it is arithmetic, not sentiment. From LAMPWORK's furniture
DTC analysis: *"A sofa lasts 7–15 years, so for most furniture brands LTV often equals a
single purchase."* Add the physical costs the same source cites — furniture return processing
at **$55–90+ per item**, white-glove delivery adding **$100–300 per item**, and home goods
carrying the highest handling fees of any vertical.

So: one purchase per customer per decade, against bulky freight and a damage rate. Customer
acquisition cost must be recovered **on the first and only order**. There is no second purchase
to amortise it against.

Venture capital is a promise to buy growth. In a category with no repeat purchase, bought
growth cannot be paid back — it can only be re-bought. That makes capital an accelerant rather
than fuel, which is exactly the shape of the three deaths above: Interior Define expanded into
a pandemic boom and could not shrink when it ended; Model No. bought machines whose returns
never arrived; Burrow reached nine-figure revenue and still got rolled up.

LAMPWORK's own summary: *"Furniture didn't kill DTC brands because the brands were bad. It
killed the ones whose unit economics couldn't survive bulky freight, a damaged-on-arrival rate,
and a customer who buys a sofa once a decade."*

### The finding that cuts the founder's way

**Made-to-order is a survivor trait in this cohort, not a killer.** Maiden Home, Inside Weather
and Sabai are all made-to-order and all alive; the same source's survivor profile is
*"made-to-order or low-inventory; profitable/bootstrapped; trade/designer channels; adding
physical retail."* Made-to-order means no inventory to write down, which is precisely the
cushion the over-funded rows did not have.

Interior Define was made-to-order and died anyway — but it died on the capital-and-demand
cliff, not on the model. The model is not what is being indicted here.

## What this says about the founder's stated idea

The idea is D2C configurable furniture made to the customer's own measurements. Against this
cohort:

1. **The configurator and the made-to-order manufacturing are on the surviving side of the
   ledger.** That part is not the risk.
2. **The venture-scale D2C ambition is on the dead side, without exception above $20M.** The
   "if you got D2C for it to be a huge startup" clause is the specific thing that has a 0-for-3
   record at that funding level.
3. **Owning a factory raises the fixed cost base into a category with a demand cliff.** Model
   No. is the direct instance — Raub's stated cause was that the machines were expensive to
   buy, maintain and modify, at seven years into a fund's ten-year clock.
4. **Two live macro facts make 2026 worse than the cohort's own decade, not better:** US
   existing-home sales at a 30-year low of roughly 4.06M in 2025, and Section 232 tariffs of
   **25% on upholstered wood furniture and cabinets effective 14 Oct 2025**. The second is a
   direct hit on any plan to serve US customers from Turkish capacity, and nothing in the
   current thesis has priced it.

## Limits

- **n = 10.** Enough to separate 10% from 40%, not enough for a decimal place.
- **Four of ten acquisition prices are undisclosed** (Burrow, Floyd, The Inside, Model No.).
  They are classed `rolled up` / `distressed` on qualitative signals — a consolidator buying a
  brand, staff not transferring, a CEO departing — not on price. If any of those four sold above
  its raise, the VC-bar rate rises.
- **Interior Define's total raised is disputed 3x** between aggregators ($26.2M vs $84.3M), and
  named rounds sum to $53M, which reconciles with neither. Its ~$96M liabilities figure comes
  from search summaries of the ABC filing, not from the filing.
- **Model No.'s raise was not found anywhere**, so it sits outside the capital-sorted table.
- Funding figures are aggregator data throughout except Joybird, which is SEC-filed.
- The cohort was enumerated from trade press and aggregator category pages, not a single
  database query. Companies that raised, failed quietly and were never written about are
  missing — which biases the survival rate **upward**. The true rate is likely worse than 30%.
