### Statement

We believe **kitchen and bath dealers, interior designers and design-build remodelers**
lose or delay jobs that fall outside their catalogue sizes because **the only alternative is
a local custom shop where a designer spends roughly thirteen hours measuring, drawing,
revising and engineering the job before a firm price exists**, and **language models plus
constraint solvers can now take a room's real dimensions to an engineered, machine-ready,
priced cabinet run** — which makes a supplier who returns a firm custom price in ten minutes
viable now.

### Components

- Segment: kitchen & bath dealers, interior designers and design-build remodelers in one US
  metro — the trade channel that specifies fitted cabinetry, not the homeowner.
- Problem: out-of-catalogue jobs are lost, discounted, or pushed onto an eight-to-twelve-week
  local shop, because pricing and engineering a one-off run is two days of skilled labour.
- Mechanism: 52% of the human labour in a custom cabinet business is design and engineering,
  not making. Remove it and the same machines sell a custom kitchen at a semi-custom price.
- Why now: a constraint solver over clearances, code and standard box widths, plus a language
  model that turns a customer's words and a homeowner-taken laser measurement into a storage
  allocation, is a 2024-2026 capability. Neither half worked reliably before.
- Existing workaround: the dealer quotes the catalogue line and takes the filler-strip
  compromise, sends the job to a local shop, or walks away from it.
- Plausible buyer: the dealer or remodeler, who resells at their own margin — not the
  homeowner. The homeowner-direct configurator is a later door, not this one.
- Cheapest next test: build a list of ~70 trade targets in one metro and call ten of them
  with one question — "when a job doesn't fit your catalogue sizes, what do you do?" — and
  write the answers down verbatim without pitching.
- Hunch disconfirmation: if the majority of ten dealers say their existing line handles
  out-of-catalogue work, or that the loss is rare and cheap, the wedge is not there.

### Evidence

- Supports: E1, E2, E3, E6 — all desk measurement. No buyer has been asked anything yet.
- Contradicts: none logged.
- Still unknown: everything on the demand side. The design-labour split (E4, E5) is derived
  from the founder's own build model, not observed in a live shop.

### Relationship to belief

- How this hunch expresses the belief: it names one channel (the trade), one product (fitted
  frameless kitchens) and one observable loss (out-of-catalogue jobs) for the belief's general
  claim that design labour, not manufacturing, is what makes custom expensive.
- Evidence that would threaten only this hunch: dealers confirming the design bottleneck but
  refusing to buy through a supplier they cannot visit, or insisting on a showroom display
  before specifying. That moves the channel, not the belief.
- Evidence that would threaten the underlying belief: shops reporting design is a small share
  of their cost, or that existing cabinet software already removes it.

We believe **structural and miscellaneous steel fabricators bidding project work** lose

- Segment: structural and misc steel fabricators bidding project work. Cited, not interviewed.
- Problem: manual takeoff, 4-8 hrs per mid-size package, explicitly named "the main bottleneck

- Mechanism: read the drawing automatically, produce takeoff and a priced bid.
- Why now: partial. Automated takeoff tools are appearing now - which is both the support and
  the threat, since it may already be being solved by others.
- Existing workaround: read the drawing, do takeoff by hand, guess yield, add operations line
  by line; or hire more estimators.
- Plausible buyer: fabricator owner or chief estimator. Untested.
- Hunch disconfirmation: if 3 of 5 fabricators say takeoff is not the binding constraint, or
  that existing automated takeoff already works well enough, this dissolves.

- Contradicts: the founder's own preferences, on two axes. Metal, not wood (against Theobald's
  materials argument). And selling INTO someone else's factory, which is a direct rejection of
  belief link 2 rather than an expression of it.
- Still unknown: whether any fabricator would pay, and whether the emerging AI-takeoff vendors
  have already taken this.

### Statement

We believe **commercial and custom millwork shops whose work is majority one-off** still do
the design-to-machine step by hand because **the affordable cabinet software solves standard
boxes and stops at custom** - parts placed by typing x, y, z coordinates, materials allocated
room by room on paper - and **a tool that engineers a non-standard job to machine-ready parts**
would remove the last manual step in an otherwise automated chain.

### Components

- Segment: commercial/custom millwork shops, 80%+ one-off product. Affected role: owner or
  CNC programmer. Evidenced by one practitioner, unprompted.
- Problem: "Mozaik is great for boxes, when you get into custom building, it is garbage...
  80% of my company's business is custom, 1 off product. It is horrendous to custom build
  with." Plus manual material allocation across a 25-room, 20-material job.
- Mechanism: automate placement and material allocation for non-standard jobs, the one
  required condition still false in the why-now table.
- Why now: unknown. The general why-now is dead - the capability is 25 years old and $125/mo
  for standard work. Whether LLMs plus solvers cross the CUSTOM threshold is untested.
- Existing workaround: owns Mozaik and works around it - 30 years of accumulated effort, the
  strongest switching-cost signal in the run.
- Plausible buyer: unknown. Nobody said this costs money rather than time.
- Hunch disconfirmation: if 4 of 6 custom millwork shops describe coordinate entry and
  material allocation as an annoyance rather than a cost, or say they would not pay to remove
  it, this dissolves. Also dissolves if shops build so heterogeneously that no tool
  generalises - the rational-avoidance case the segment card raised and could not settle.

### Evidence

- Supports: the cncpgmr verbatims in recon/2026-08-31/corpus-pool.json. n=1.
- Contradicts: dconti ("It is a cabinet to cnc program it does that very well"), Ryan and
  Stephen Williams - all satisfied Mozaik users. Their work is standard, which is why this
  hunch is scoped to custom, but the split is asserted from one person's account.
- Still unknown: everything about money. Segment size, spend, reachability, willingness to
  pay - none established.

### Statement

We believe **the organisations that currently pay to dispose of furniture** are throwing away
material with recoverable value because **remanufacturing is labour-heavy, the input is wildly
variable, and the industry has therefore stayed manual and price-capped for 77 years**, and
**computer vision plus robotics for inspection, disassembly, defect detection and refinishing**
could make automated remanufacturing economic for the first time.

### Components

- Segment: unknown. Candidates: office facilities managers, junk removal operators, municipal
  waste authorities, office furniture liquidators. Narrowing this is the first job of the calls.
- Problem: 12.1M tons of US furniture discarded annually, 80.1% landfilled, 19.5% burned,
  0.3% recycled. ~$652M in tipping fees alone. Consumers pay $75-700 per junk removal.
- Mechanism: the input variability that keeps remanufacturing manual is a perception and
  handling problem - which is what vision and robotics got good at in 2024-26.
- Why now: UNVERIFIED. The honest question is why 77 years of remanufacturers did not automate,
  and whether the answer is "could not" or "not yet".
- Existing workaround: landfill, energy recovery, donation (Goodwill, Habitat ReStore), paid
  junk removal (1-800-GOT-JUNK, LoadUp, Remoov).
- Plausible buyer: unknown. Note the input may carry NEGATIVE cost - collection is already a
  paid service - which changes the economics materially if it holds.
- Hunch disconfirmation: if 3 of 5 remanufacturers say input variability is not what keeps them
  manual, or that labour is not the binding cost, the mechanism is wrong. Also dies if the
  recoverable fraction is too small: most discarded furniture is MDF/particleboard, which cannot
  be recycled into new board at all.

### Evidence

- Supports: the waste numbers (12.1M tons, 0.3% recycled); potentially negative-cost input;
  office furniture passes all four tests that make pallet recycling work - standardised,
  aggregated, short-haul, gradeable.
- Contradicts: Kaiyo raised ~$50m to do the EASIER version - collect and resell, no
  remanufacturing - and wound down in 2024 on "high logistics costs, the complexity of handling
  used goods, and cyclical demand". MDF and particleboard cannot be recycled into new boards;
  most post-consumer MDF is incinerated. The remanufactured office furniture industry is 77
  years old, sells at ~80% off new, and is small enough that no market-size figure is published.
- Still unknown: everything a buyer would say. Zero conversations held.
