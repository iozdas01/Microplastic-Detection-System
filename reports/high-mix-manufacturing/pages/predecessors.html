Predecessors and Survivors

Every company found that tried to industrialise made-to-order production across 120 years — who died, who is still running, what actually killed the ones that failed, and where that leaves a way in.

The belief under test

"The problem is that software and machines don't talk, and you have to own both to fix it."

This is belief v2, which this run tested on 2026-08-31. It was superseded on 2026-09-03 by v3 — "manufacturing will be done through an app, and interoperability is the precondition for it" — which demotes "own both ends" from the belief to one implementation option. The page is left as the record of what was actually tested; read the conclusions about link 2 as conclusions about an option, not about the belief.

Two links: (1) the coupling is broken — first-hand, measured at Cambridge. (2) therefore you must own both ends — not observed, arrived as advice. The graveyard speaks mostly to link 2.

The finding that reframes everything

Batch-size-one manufacturing with automated quoting is not a new capability. It is a 1999 business.

Protolabs has done this for twenty-five years: upload a CAD file, software analyses manufacturability and returns a firm price in minutes, then generates toolpaths and makes the part in roughly a million square feet of factories it owns. One to 200 parts, 1–3 business days.

That is the belief's mechanism — software and machines talking, one company owning both ends — already built, public, and profitable. It just happened in plastic and metal rather than wood.

So the why-now claim doesn't survive as written

"LLMs plus constraint solvers make this newly possible" is false. The honest question is not why is this possible now but why did wood and panel goods never get what metal got in 1999? That question is unanswered and is the highest-value thing to go and find out.

Owning a factory does not fix CAC

Casper reached an IPO and still returned less than it raised, and it had nothing to do with who owned the machines — it used contract manufacturers throughout. Interior Define died asset-light; Model No. died asset-heavy. Keep "this company has an acquisition-cost problem" strictly separate from "this belief is wrong" — they are different failures with different fixes.

Who lived — the counter-query

The first pass of this run searched only for failures, found a graveyard, and concluded the graveyard was fatal. Asking the opposite question inverted the answer.

$229M Xometry Q2 2026: revenue $229.3m, +41% YoY, marketplace +45%. FY2026 guidance raised to 27–28% growth (an earlier "33–34%" here was wrong). Owns no factories.
$149M Protolabs quarterly revenue, +10.6%. ~$533m annual, $1.45bn market cap. Gross margin 44%. Owns ~1m sq ft.

What that actually means for link 2

The company that owns no machines is larger and growing roughly four times faster. The company that owns them earns more per dollar and is cheaper at 1–10 parts.

Both are alive. So "you have to own both ends" is not a necessity claim — it is an advantage claim, bounded to low volume. That partially satisfies the founder's own disconfirmer #1, which was written before this was known.

Japan: the one place industrialised housing worked

Daiwa House (1955), Sekisui House (1960) and Toyota Home (1977) succeeded at exactly what Katerra, Lustron and Veev failed at. Why:

  • Overwhelming demand pull — postwar Japan was short ~4.2 million homes and construction labour could not keep pace.
  • They were spin-offs of existing manufacturers seeking new markets for products they already made — Daiwa for steel tubes, Sekisui for plastics, Toyota for its production system.
  • The Toyota Production System was purpose-built for high-mix, low-volume — efficiently producing small numbers of many different products. Precisely the problem shape.
  • They went premium, not cheap. Japanese prefab became higher quality than traditional custom building, with high customisation.

The uncomfortable contrast

Every Western attempt tried to be cheaper than conventional building. The successful Japanese firms became better and more expensive. If you build a factory to undercut a local shop on price, you are repeating Lustron and Katerra. If you build one to do something a local shop cannot do at all, you are repeating Sekisui.

Furniture manufacturing itself

The first pass never examined this. It matters, because it is the industry the founder wants to enter.

North Carolina was the largest producer of both upholstered and wooden household furniture in the United States. Between 1999 and 2009 it lost more than half its furniture manufacturing jobs.

The cause was not automation failure or a broken digital thread. It was the US–China Bilateral WTO Agreement of November 1999, which lowered tariff barriers and opened the door to far cheaper Asian labour. American retailers under price pressure sourced overseas; NC manufacturers carrying higher wages and legacy costs could not compete.

China undercut North Carolina like the state's furniture makers had undercut competitors in New England and Michigan. Richmond Fed, economic history of the NC furniture industry

What this says about the wedge

US furniture manufacturing did not lose because nobody automated the design-to-machine handoff. It lost on labour-cost arbitrage. Automation changes that calculus only where the product cannot be shipped cheaply from Asia — which points at fitted, measured-to-the-room, fast-turnaround goods, and away from catalogue furniture that containerises well.

That is an argument for cabinets and millwork over freestanding furniture — and it is a different argument from the one in the original belief.

The pattern

Zero wrong-time failures

Across 120 years and every company above, not one died because the technology was not ready. Every diagnosable failure was wrong-idea: demand, cost, delivery, financing, or operating leverage.

The method card is explicit — wrong-idea predecessors are counterevidence, not timing proof. This graveyard does not say "now is the moment." It says the hard part was never the technology.

Outcomes verified 2026-09-03. Every company outcome in the tables above was re-checked against a primary or trade source and each now carries its citation. Three claims changed: Casper's "~$300 lost per mattress" had no source and was removed (the $1.1bn-to-$286m outcome is verifiable and makes the same point); Veev's "~170 units built" had no source and was removed, and its ending was a Lennar acquisition rather than a plain liquidation; Xometry's guidance figure was wrong — FY2026 is 27–28%, not 33–34% — and its quarterly numbers now say which quarter; and Fab.com's exit was to PCH International at a reported $15–50m in stock, not a flat $15m, with its “Vitra-backed” successor claim unverified. Three diagram labels contradicted the tables beside them and were corrected: Shapeways relaunched in Dec 2024, Veev was acquired, and Fab sold in 2015 rather than 2016. The pattern to carry away is failed returns, not failed companies — several of these still trade under new owners, which is a harder fact for a founder than "they all died".

The recurring cause of death is operating leverage: fixed factory cost against lumpy, geographically dispersed, project-shaped demand. Lustron, Katerra, Veev and Shapeways all failed on it — Katerra and Lustron outright, Veev into an acquisition, Shapeways into Chapter 7 and a later relaunch. Shapeways is the sharpest case — 21 million parts delivered to a million customers in 160 countries, and it still could not fund payroll.

This belief optimises the front office. The graveyard died in the back.

The one voice in the whole run

Everything above is desk research. Exactly one practitioner described the belief's mechanism failing, unprompted, in their own words — a thirty-year commercial millwork veteran on WOODWEB:

Mozaik is great for boxes, when you get into custom building, it is garbage… 80% of my company's business is custom, 1 off product. It is horrendous to custom build with. cncpgmr, WOODWEB CNC forum
I have a job with 25 rooms, and 20 different materials… I have to figure out what I need for that room, write them down, and make sure I only use materials needed for that room. cncpgmr — manual material allocation, exactly in scope

And directly against it, from the same thread, a satisfied user of 2.5 years:

It is a cabinet to cnc program it does that very well. It covers all different types of construction methods and it will adapt to the way YOU build cabinets. dconti, WOODWEB CNC forum

n = 1

The strongest pro-belief evidence in this entire run rests on one person. Nobody said it costs them money rather than time. And the segment card raised an objection it could not settle: if every shop builds differently — "it will adapt to the way YOU build cabinets" — the thing being automated may not generalise across shops.

The map

Every company above, placed by what material they worked and how made-to-order the product actually was. Colour is outcome. The point of the picture is the gap it leaves.

What the map answers

Was there demand for custom furniture?

Look at the wood band, left and middle. Made.com, Casper, Interior Define, Fab/Hem, Model No. — none of them returned their capital, all selling catalogue or lightly configurable furniture to consumers. Two were not deaths: Casper listed and was taken private, Model No. was absorbed. The pattern is failed returns, not failed companies, and that is the more damning version. The only survivors in that column are Barker and CabinetNow, who sell custom-sized cabinet boxes, not furniture.

Reading: demand for custom freestanding furniture has killed everyone who chased it. Demand for fitted, measured-to-the-room product supports live businesses. That matches your own E1/E2 measurement — and it is the opposite of Theobald's furniture-first advice.

What were they doing?

Three distinct plays. Metal parts: automate the quote, then either own the factory (Protolabs) or broker it (Xometry). Both work. Wood: mostly build a consumer brand and outsource production — which is what died. Buildings: vertically integrate everything, which failed everywhere except where the Toyota Production System was already in the building.

How to get in

The dashed box. Wood, engineered-per-order, is occupied only by a UK operator (Cutwrights, 2009), a Dutch marketplace (Cutr), a size-bounded US service (SendCutSend, ≤30×44in), and a small open network (Opendesk).

Nobody is doing carcass-scale, engineered-per-order panel work in the US. That is either a real opening or a reason nobody bothered — and the map cannot tell you which.

Read the gap honestly

An empty quadrant is not a market. Three of the four cells nearest it contain companies that stayed small — which is at least as consistent with "the economics do not support a big business here" as with "nobody has tried properly." The map generates the question; only the phone calls answer it.

What to do next

Five phone calls

Call five commercial millwork shops doing majority one-off work. One question: "walk me through what happens between the approved drawing and the machine." Do not say software, AI, or automation.

If they describe typing coordinates and allocating materials by hand — and then say what it costs them — the pocket is real. If they shrug, it is an annoyance, and the belief has no pocket left.

Second question, worth as much: why did panel goods never get what metal got in 1999?
