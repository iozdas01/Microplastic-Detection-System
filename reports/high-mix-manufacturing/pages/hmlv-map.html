The consumer map

Not companies this time — categories, scored on what actually makes a factory-in-a-showroom work. Every proven example clears the same two tests.

Why the top-right works

LensCrafters, 1983: on-site grinding lab, "glasses in about an hour", ~900 in-store labs. By 1989 — 350 stores, $532M sales and $30M net income, roughly 40% of its parent's entire operating earnings.

CEREC: ~$150k per site, 38,000+ practices, 50% less chair time, 35% higher practice profitability. That is the replication maths working.

Why the bottom-left dies

Taste-driven spec, shipped from a plant. The customer does the specifying work — and the mass-customisation research says that effort itself destroys the price premium. Then you pay CAC on top. Casper lost ~$300 a unit.

The trap in the top-left

Build-A-Bear does not operate any factories — $486M revenue, ~$70M operating income, 77% of goods from five vendors. Manufacturing-as-theatre works while the manufacturing is fake. If the customisation is taste rather than fit, an autonomous factory is expensive set dressing.

The bottom-right problem

Closets, cabinets, countertops are properly measured — but the machine is too big for a showroom and the job takes days. That is where fitted furniture actually sits, and it is why this is a distribution business rather than a same-visit one.

The industry runs on centralised plants shipping to installers, not showroom factories.

The one thing with no precedent: autonomy

LensCrafters has technicians. CEREC has a dentist. Every working example on this map has a skilled person between the customer and the machine. "Fully autonomous" is the part of the vision nobody has done — which makes it either the actual innovation, or the reason it hasn't happened.

Why the UK has this market and the US doesn't

Three separate searches turned up the same shape: a deep UK made-to-measure market, and almost nothing equivalent in the US. That is either the opportunity or the reason to walk away — so it was worth settling.

It is structural. The UK market exists because of specific conditions the US does not share.

The bear case, and it is strong

The empty US made-to-measure market is empty for a reason. Britain buys fitted furniture because its homes are a third the size, decades older, structurally irregular, and come without any built-in storage at all. America standardised room dimensions in 1927 and puts a closet in every bedroom.

"Nobody has built Cutwrights for America" is not a gap waiting to be filled. It is a market that is smaller because the houses are different.

The nuance that saves part of it

Americans do buy fitted storage — roughly $6bn a year in custom closets, ~14 million households installing or upgrading. So the demand is not absent. The job is different:

  • UK job: "I have no storage. Build me some." — the whole object is bespoke
  • US job: "My closet exists but the inside of it is bad." — a fitted interior in a standard opening

That second job is a genuinely different product: fewer variables, more repeatable, standard depths and heights, varying mainly in width and configuration. Which is better for an automated factory, not worse — and it is why Barker, CabinetNow, California Closets and the Container Store all survive in the US while the mail-order custom-furniture brands died.

And US housing is ageing

The median US home is now 42 years old, up from 31 in 2005, and 47% are at least 45 years old — up from 39% in 2014. Older stock is less standard. This is a slow tailwind, not a reason on its own, but it points the right way.

What the whole map says

1. Both asset models work

Xometry brokers and Protolabs owns; both are large and growing. Ownership is not a necessity, it is a margin choice — 44% vs 35.7%. Shapeways sat between the two and died.

2. Consumer custom only works two ways

Surface personalisation, or fitted-and-installed. Engineered geometry shipped as a parcel is a graveyard, and the academic research explains why: buyers won't pay a premium, and the specification effort is itself a cost.

3. The demand signal is on the B2B side

One company 35,000 overtime hours past capacity, spending $250M to expand, funded by Sequoia because it was turning orders away. Driver: AI physical infrastructure — data centres, robotics, aerospace, defence.

4. Every incumbent stops at the same place

Rule-based DFM rejects threads, internal radii, undercuts, thin walls and special tolerances into a 2–24 hour manual queue. "Often rejecting features that skilled machinists could easily manufacture."

Which hunch this points at

Nothing on this map supports a consumer furniture play. Two things it does support: the rejected-geometry slice on the B2B side, and fitted-and-installed on the consumer side, which is the only consumer quadrant with living companies in it.

Cheapest test of the first: upload a set of deliberately awkward parts to SendCutSend, OSH Cut and Xometry and count how many instant-quote versus get kicked to manual. An afternoon, no interviews, direct measurement of the gap.
