# H3 — the pitch

There are two of these and they are not the same document. Using the second one where the
first belongs is the single most common way a discovery conversation produces nothing.

- **The discovery pitch** earns a conversation and contains **no solution**. Its only job is
  to make someone comfortable telling you about a bad week they had.
- **The thesis pitch** answers "so what are you building?" — from an advisor, an investor, or
  the contact themselves once they have already told you the truth.

Use the second only when asked. If you volunteer it, everything after it is contaminated:
people stop reporting what happened and start reacting to your idea, politely.

---

## 1 · The discovery pitch

### The shape

Three sentences and a question. Anything longer invites a monologue back and you learn nothing.

> I'm a mechanical engineer, I've been around manufacturing since I was thirteen, and I'm
> trying to understand how made-to-measure actually works in practice rather than on paper.
> Not the sales side, the bit where a number gets taken off a window and ends up driving a
> machine.
>
> Can I ask you about the last one that came back the wrong size?

That is the whole thing. Note what is absent: no AI, no robotics, no automation, no product,
no company, no "I'm building". You are a curious engineer, which is true, and it is the most
disarming thing you can be.

### Why this opener and not a better-sounding one

- **It names a specific event, not a topic.** "How do you handle custom sizing?" gets you a
  process description, which is fiction. "The last one that came back wrong" gets you a story,
  which is data.
- **It pre-admits the thing is normal.** By asking about *the last one* you have already
  signalled you know it happens, so they do not have to decide whether to admit it.
- **It puts you on the machine side, not the buyer side.** They will assume you understand
  tolerances and they will skip the simplifications.

### The follow-ups, in order, one at a time

Do not fire these as a list. Each one only unlocks if the previous got a real answer.

1. **"Whose was it, in the end?"** — customer mismeasure, your survey, the manufacturer, or
   the window being out of square. This is the causal split the recon could not get.
2. **"What did it cost you to put right?"** — let them compute. Sun Glow's published number is
   that at 40% margin one remake wipes 1.5 similar jobs; you want to know whether working
   fitters recognise that or laugh at it.
3. **"How long did the replacement take?"** — the reviews cluster on 3-4 weeks to six months.
   Turnaround may be the actual pain rather than the cost.
4. **"Out of the last hundred, roughly how many?"** — **the number the whole hunch rests on.**
   Ask it last, and ask it as a range. "Roughly, one in fifty? One in ten?" People who will not
   produce a percentage will happily correct a wrong one.
5. **"Who else should I ask?"**

### If they ask what you're building — before you have the answers

Deflect once, honestly, and go back to them:

> Honestly, still working that out. I keep finding that the number is the fragile part, so
> right now I'm just trying to understand how often it actually breaks. What's your sense?

If they push a second time, they have earned it. Give them section 2, briefly, and then stop
talking.

## 2 · The thesis pitch

For advisors, investors, and contacts who have already told you the truth.

> A made-to-measure product is a number that has to travel from a window to a machine.
> It gets taken by a person, typed into an order system, passed to a manufacturer, and cut.
> Nobody in that chain can check it against the actual opening until the finished thing turns
> up at the house — and by then a wrong number costs the full product and the full labour,
> twice, collected once.
>
> The industry already prices this as a known cost. SelectBlinds gives you a free remake when
> *you* measured wrong, offered at checkout. Sun Glow tells its dealers that one remake wipes
> out one and a half similar jobs. Nobody publishes the rate.
>
> I spent a year at Cambridge measuring exactly this class of handoff — annotating DXF at 100%
> F1 and STEP at 74% — and two years before that running a high-mix precision shop where the
> same number got retyped between CAD, the machines and the ERP. My bet is that you fix it by
> owning the path from the measurement to the machine, not by adding another integration to it.

**Why this version works on this audience:** it opens with a mechanism rather than a market,
it evidences the pain with someone else's behaviour rather than your opinion, it is honest
that the rate is unknown, and the credential arrives last as support rather than first as a
claim.

**Do not say "vibe manufacturing"** to anyone in the trade. It is a good internal name and it
will lose you a fitter in one sentence.

## 3 · Where the furniture retailers fit

The founder's instinct to test both is right and worth stating explicitly: **the pitch does
not change.** A furniture or interiors retailer selling made-to-measure anything has the same
measure-to-machine chain and the same failure mode. Use the identical opener with one word
swapped:

> ...the bit where a number gets taken off a **room** and ends up driving a machine.

Testing both segments against one question is what makes the segment question answerable.
Whichever group produces the sharper rate and the clearer "we eat it" is the segment; running
them separately with different pitches would tell you nothing about which.

---

## 4 · The traps, specific to this lane

- **Do not ask about "the industry".** Ask about their last one. The recon already has the
  industry view and it is exactly what cannot be sized.
- **Do not accept "it hardly ever happens" as an answer.** Follow with "when was the most
  recent?" A rare event people can date is not rare.
- **Do not lead with the free-remake guarantees.** If you mention SelectBlinds' FIT Protection
  they will discuss SelectBlinds' policy rather than their own losses.
- **Beware the supplier-blaming reflex.** Fitters blame manufacturers and manufacturers blame
  fitters. Both are reporting honestly from where they stand, and the causal split only comes
  from asking both.
- **A designer or specifier is not a buyer here.** They pick the product and never carry the
  remake. Useful for vocabulary and introductions; logging their answers as pain evidence is
  the out-of-competence failure the segment gate exists to prevent.

## 5 · The investor pitch — added 2026-09-03 for a specific conversation

Section 2 is for an advisor who will argue with the mechanism. This one is for someone
deciding whether to fund it, and the difference is not tone — it is that **the arithmetic
gets checked**. Everything below is in the ledger; the citations are there so a challenge
can be answered with a source rather than a recollection.

### The trap to walk around first

The instinct is to open with the size of the blinds market. Do not. `E13` already ran that
arithmetic: price every US firm in blind manufacturing and window-treatment retail at an
affordability-anchored seat price and the ceiling is **$9.3m ARR with 100% of the market
bought, no competitor and no churn.** An investor who opens with "how big is this" and hears
"$3bn" will do that division himself and arrive at "not venture-scale" — which is the
objection the advisor already raised in the conversation `E13` was written to answer.

The blinds number is not the market. It is the **beachhead**, and its virtue is that it is
countable: 320 manufacturers nationally, ten of whom hold 62.5% of the revenue (`E12`).

### The numbers, correctly labelled

Three different denominators get called "the market" and they are not interchangeable.
Measured today from the 2022 Economic Census (`E19`):

| | US | California |
|---|---|---|
| Blind & shade **manufacturing** (NAICS 337920) | **$2.59bn**, 320 establishments | $389m, 53 establishments |
| Retail **window-treatment line**, every seller (NAPCS 5000625000) | **$9.63bn**, 65,753 establishments | — |
| The **made-to-measure pool** the wedge opens into (`E13`) | **$108.5bn** across five industries | — |
| Furniture & related product **manufacturing** (NAICS 337) | $88.69bn | $8.08bn |

Your "$3bn US / $400m California" is the first row rounded — the *making* of blinds, the
smallest number in the stack and the wrong one to lead with. The $108.5bn is the one that
matches the ambition: finish carpentry $42.97bn, wood kitchen cabinets and countertops
$20.32bn, wood windows and doors $19.13bn, glass and glazing $18.08bn, cut stone $8.00bn —
five industries that all cut to a dimension taken at a site. Blinds is 4.6% of it.

### The shape

> Every made-to-measure product starts as a number somebody takes off a wall. That number
> gets written down, typed into an order system, sent to a factory, and cut. Nothing in that
> chain can check it against the actual opening until the finished thing arrives at the
> house — and when it is wrong the product is scrap. You pay for the material twice and the
> labour twice, and you collect once.
>
> The industry already prices this. One retailer I spoke to charges **$225 a visit** to go
> and take the number themselves, on every order, and told me it is still "not accurate
> enough" — and they decline jobs outright when the drive is too far. Another sends people
> free and makes the customer legally liable if they measure it themselves. SelectBlinds
> sells you a **free-remake guarantee at checkout** for your own measuring error. Those are
> three different companies paying three different taxes on the same missing check.
>
> I want to own the path from that number to the machine, not sell software into it. I've
> got CNC capacity in Türkiye inside the EU customs union, I ran a high-mix precision shop
> for two years, and I spent a year at Cambridge measuring exactly this handoff — annotating
> DXF at 100% F1 and STEP at 74%.
>
> Blinds is where I start, because a blind is a handful of dimensions and 320 manufacturers
> is a countable market to learn in. It is not where this goes. Everything that gets cut to a
> site-taken dimension has the same broken handoff, and that pool is $108.5bn.

### Why this order

- **Mechanism before market.** The failure is physical and he can picture it. A market number
  offered first is a number to be argued with; offered after the mechanism, it is a size.
- **Three companies' behaviour, not your opinion.** $225, the liability transfer, and the
  checkout guarantee are three independent firms spending real money on the same gap. That is
  the strongest evidence in the ledger and it costs you nothing to say.
- **"Own the path, not sell software into it" is the answer to `E13`.** Of the three routes
  that survive its arithmetic — take manufacturing revenue, generalise the measure-to-machine
  path, or go global — you are proposing the first two together. Say so in those words. It
  converts your value-chain instinct from ambition into the specific reason the $9.3m ceiling
  does not apply to you.
- **The credential lands last.** It is support, not a claim.

### What to volunteer before he finds it

An investor who checks and finds something you did not say discounts everything you did. All
three of these are cheap to say and expensive to be caught on.

1. **The misfit rate is unpublished, and I have not measured it.** Nobody publishes it. Sun
   Glow tells dealers one remake wipes 1.5 similar jobs at 40% margin; a Blinds.com
   technician put the cause at roughly 50/50 between his own error and the factory's
   (`E15`). That is the number the next ten conversations exist to get.
2. **The buyer is not identified yet.** `E12` is blunt: the pain concentrates in the ten
   firms that hold 62.5% of manufacturing revenue and carry $4.6m–$15.5m of remade order
   value a year, while 27 firms at the bottom carry $1,747–$5,825 each. So this is an
   enterprise sale to about ten companies, not a product-led motion across two thousand
   dealers. And the family-run owners I spoke to were **not** enthusiastic about a software
   answer (`E10`).
3. **Blinds.com already runs the router.** They do the measure themselves and fan orders out
   to many manufacturers, with franchises for overflow (`E17`). An "open router for window
   coverings" is the incumbent's core model, not an empty gap. What is unoccupied is the
   accuracy of the number entering the router — which is why the pitch is the measurement,
   not the routing.

### Do not say

- **"Measurement caps how much of this category can sell online."** It is in the H3 statement
  and today's Census pull contradicts it (`E18`): in 2017, 31.9% of the US retail
  window-treatment line was bought from nonstore sellers — electronic shopping and mail-order
  houses — against 8.8% e-commerce across all US retail, and the two visit-heavy channels
  together came to only 24.1%. The category went remote anyway. Say "bought from a nonstore
  seller", not "e-commerce": the row classifies the seller, not the transaction, and an
  analyst will know the difference. The surviving — and better —
  version is that it went online *by paying a tax to do it*: the guarantees, the liability
  transfers and the $225 truck rolls are what the industry spends to move a physical
  measurement through a digital channel. Lead with that and the same fact becomes the reason
  the opportunity exists rather than a claim that fails a database check.
- **"$45 per visit at larger retailers."** No source in the ledger. Until it has one it is a
  recollection, and it sits next to $225 in a way that invites the question "which is it?"
- **"Vibe manufacturing."** Same rule as section 2.
- **"$3bn market"** as the opener. See the trap above.

### The ask

Decide this before the meeting, because the pitch above earns a conversation and nothing
else. If it is money, name the amount and what it buys — on current evidence the honest
answer is that it buys the ten conversations and one built path from a measured window to a
cut part, not a go-to-market. If it is his judgement on which of `E13`'s three routes to
take, ask that directly; it is a better use of him and it is the decision actually open.

### 2026-09-03 (later) · the $9.3m ARR ceiling came off the slide

### 2026-09-03 · investor / one slide · the spine changed from measurement to batch size

Forced by: founder correction to the belief framing, plus `E18`, `E13`, `E15`.

one-liner — it committed to owning the chain when that is one of two live options. "Measurement
