## Core Insight

Timing is one causal history. Earlier attempts reveal which assumptions failed;
specific technological or institutional thresholds show what changed; the
why-now claim asks whether all necessary conditions have converged. Running
these as separate lenses repeats the same predecessor search and often produces
three versions of the same conclusion.

## Process

1. Find credible predecessors: failed companies, abandoned internal efforts,
   earlier products, adjacent models, and past attempts to serve the same job.
2. Diagnose each outcome without assuming it was merely "too early":
   - pain or willingness to pay was weak;
   - technology, reliability, cost, or data was inadequate;
   - regulation or procurement blocked adoption;
   - distribution was unavailable;
   - behavior or trust had not shifted;
   - execution or financing failed for reasons unrelated to the thesis.
3. Separate **wrong-time**, **wrong-idea**, and **unknown** failures. Wrong-idea
   predecessors are counterevidence, not timing proof.
4. List every condition the active hunch requires: technical capability,
   unit cost, data access, regulation, distribution, buyer behavior, and trust.
5. For technological conditions, name the threshold rather than a trend:
   cost, accuracy, latency, reliability, power, size, or ubiquity. Cite when and
   how the threshold crossed.
6. Mark each condition `false→true`, `still false`, or `uncertain`, with dates
   and evidence. A why-now claim exists only if the blocking conditions are now
   sufficiently true together.
7. Check whether the same changes also enable incumbents and new entrants. A
   falling barrier is timing evidence, not a moat.
8. State what must still change and what evidence would show the hunch remains
   too early.

## Output

```text
Predecessor | outcome | failure cause | wrong-time/wrong-idea/unknown | source

Required condition | status | threshold/change | date | evidence | remaining risk

Why-now conclusion:
Counterevidence:
Still-too-early trigger:
```

## Example

An earlier insurance startup may have faced a multi-year custom build, sparse
property data, and low consumer trust. A later attempt can claim better timing
only after showing that infrastructure reduced build cost, external datasets
became sufficiently complete, and buyers became willing to trust a new brand.
If demand was weak in the predecessor, cheaper infrastructure does not rescue
the thesis.

## Limitations

- Failed companies are hard to research and public post-mortems simplify causes.
- Founders can rationalize any trend as a threshold after choosing an idea.
- Conditions often change gradually; exact crossing dates may remain uncertain.
- Convergence lowers barriers for competitors too.
- This method tests timing, not pain magnitude or willingness to pay.

## Connection to the Loop

Run once for the timing family after the interpretation outputs exist. Examine
the active hunch's mechanism and why-now claim, supplying predecessor
counterevidence and explicit conditions that later validation can monitor.
