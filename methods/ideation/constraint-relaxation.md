## Core Insight

Founders unconsciously design within *today's* limits — bandwidth, cost per unit, latency, model accuracy, battery — which quietly caps ambition to incremental products. Constraint Relaxation deliberately lifts a binding constraint in imagination ("assume broadband is universal," "assume inference is free," "assume the sensor is tiny and cheap") and designs the product that the relaxed world enables, then works *backward* to what you can ship now that folds the ambition in as the constraint actually lifts.

The mechanism: technology moves along curves, so the constraint that's binding today is often gone in three to ten years. If you design for the relaxed world, you're building toward where the puck is going rather than where it is. The critical discipline is *which* constraints you're allowed to relax — cost, reliability, ubiquity, and speed all move on curves and are fair game. The laws of physics do not: relax those and you get antigravity and perpetual motion, not a startup.

**Relationship to neighboring methods.** This is the forward-looking twin of [Why-Now and Predecessor Analysis](why-now-predecessor-analysis.md): that method looks *backward* to identify thresholds already crossed and conditions that converged; this one looks *forward*, imagining a constraint gone and then backcasting. Use them as a pair — relax to imagine, then verify which constraints have genuinely lifted. It removes *technical and economic* constraints in imagination, not psychological filters around ideas a founder considers messy or boring.

## Process

1. State the end goal with no limits: what is the science-fiction version of this product?
2. Identify the specific constraint blocking it today (bandwidth, cost, accuracy, latency, battery, regulation).
3. Ask: if that constraint disappeared, what would we build? Design for that world.
4. Sanity-check against physics — you may relax cost, ubiquity, and reliability, never physical law. Keep the horizon to 3–10 years; what's knowable in 50 isn't relevant to today's company.
5. Backcast: what can you build *now* that is genuinely valuable today and folds the ambitious version in as the constraint lifts?
6. For hard-tech ambitions, ask "what's the closest analog to what I want, and where do I find the people who can relax this constraint?"

## Example

At Idealab in 1996–2000 (Howard Morgan), most people had 56k dial-up, but teams that *assumed* broadband designed rich interfaces, GPS, and data-heavy applications that became viable the moment the constraint lifted — they weren't scrambling to catch up. Tesla is the backcasting half: the ambition was mass-market electric vehicles, but the shippable-now product was the Roadster, a low-volume high-end car that funded the path toward the relaxed-cost future. Same pattern — design for the world where the constraint is gone, ship the wedge that's viable while it's still there.

## Limitations

- Relaxing physics or hard regulatory limits produces unbuildable fantasies, not opportunities.
- Timing risk: you can be right about the future and still too early if the constraint lifts slower than you bet — pair with Why-Now and Predecessor Analysis to check the actual curve.
- Backcasting to a valuable "now" product is the hard part. A vision with no shippable wedge is a research project, not a startup.
- Says nothing about demand — the relaxed-world product still needs its pain and market assumptions tested.

## Connection to the Loop

During a shotgun, relax a binding constraint inside the active hunch and report
whether its mechanism or why-now becomes more or less credible. Do not silently
replace the hunch with the relaxed-world idea. It feeds Why-Now and Predecessor
Analysis, which verifies whether the constraint has actually crossed a
threshold.
