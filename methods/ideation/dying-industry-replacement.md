## Core Insight

When an industry is dying or structurally broken, it creates a gap that something will fill. Startups "garbage-collect" broken industries. The non-obvious insight: **the replacement never comes directly**. It comes in from the side, along an axis the incumbent didn't expect.

PG: "Don't look for a replacement for x. Look for something that people will *later say* turned out to be a replacement for x."

Journalism isn't replaced by better journalism. It's replaced along multiple axes simultaneously: information delivery (Twitter, newsletters), time-killing (social media, YouTube), writer income (Substack), advertising vehicle (programmatic, social ads). Each replacement attacked one axis and ignored the others. None of them set out to "replace journalism."

The method: find a dying or broken industry, map all the axes it serves, and ask what product people will *in retrospect* say replaced each axis — even if that product doesn't currently think of itself as being in that industry.

## Process

1. Identify an industry that is dying, structurally broken, or widely disliked by its own customers
2. Map every axis the industry currently serves:
   - What job does it do for buyers? (functional, emotional, social)
   - How does it make money?
   - What problem does it solve for suppliers/participants?
   - What's the underlying need that won't disappear even if the industry does?
3. For each axis, ask: what would a product look like that served *only that axis*, better, without the dead weight of the incumbent model?
4. Specifically look for axes the industry serves *badly* but where customers have no alternative — those are the highest-value replacement axes
5. Use `ideation/segment-wedge-map.md` to check incumbent posture toward the replacement axis and whether the ignored segment has credible economics.

## Example

**Journalism:** serves readers (information, entertainment), writers (income, attention), advertisers (targeted audience). Traditional journalism replaced on each axis by different products: Twitter/newsletters (fast information), YouTube/TikTok (entertainment/time), Substack/paid communities (writer income), programmatic ads (advertiser targeting). None of them said "we are replacing journalism."

**Taxis:** served riders (point-to-point transport), served drivers (flexible income). Uber attacked the rider axis (convenience, pricing, quality) and accidentally created a new driver axis (gig income). Taxis didn't see it as competition — they saw it as "an app."

## Limitations

- Dying industries are obvious to spot but the replacement axis is usually non-obvious — the method generates hypotheses, not answers
- Incumbents fight harder than expected when their core revenue stream is threatened — the replacement may be right but the transition slow
- Some industries die because the underlying need disappears (horse-drawn carriages), not because a better solution exists — no replacement opportunity there
- Works better for consumer/SMB markets than regulated industries where replacement requires regulatory change

## Connection to the Loop

During a shotgun, ask whether the active hunch actually addresses a durable job
on the industry's replacement axis. Keep other axes as out-of-scope
alternatives. Outside the shotgun, use the method to find candidate markets in
transition or to reframe a thesis after evidence.
