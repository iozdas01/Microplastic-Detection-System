## Core Insight

Most startup ideas die not because the market is wrong but because the entry surface is too large. When early evidence shows low adoption, scattered buying behavior, or an unclear first customer, the instinct is to broaden the product. The correct move is usually the opposite: narrow the entry point to the smallest segment that (1) has the pain most acutely, (2) can be reached without needing the rest of the market, and (3) whose successful use makes the adjacent segment reachable.

The wedge works because it gives you a beachhead with defensible density before you expand. Stripe started with developer-friendly payment integration, not enterprise billing. HubSpot started with inbound marketing for SMBs, not CRM at scale. The wedge isn't a smaller version of your vision — it's the specific door that opens the building.

Wedge Narrowing is the mutation you make when evidence says: "the pain is real, but our go-to-market is diffuse and we're not winning anyone decisively."

## Process

1. Map all the customer segments currently in your thesis — by role, industry, company size, or use-case.
2. For each segment, score on three dimensions:
   - **Pain intensity**: how much does this segment suffer without the solution? (1–5)
   - **Reachability**: how directly can you reach and sell to them right now? (1–5)
   - **Expansion leverage**: if you win this segment, does it make the next segment easier? (1–5)
3. Multiply: `pain × reachability × leverage`. The highest-scoring segment is your wedge.
4. Rewrite the thesis to focus exclusively on that segment — new ICP, new message, new first metric.
5. Design a test: what does "winning" this segment look like in 60 days? Set a threshold.

## Example

**Stripe**: The initial market was "anyone who needs to take payments online." The wedge was developers who wanted to integrate payments without dealing with banks — a tiny but reachable segment who would spread the product virally through their networks. Winning developers created the pathway to winning SMBs, then enterprise.

**Slack**: The wedge was remote software teams who needed async communication. Not "teams" broadly — specifically technical teams using IRC-style channels. That segment spread to adjacent teams within the same companies.

## Limitations

- A wedge that has no natural expansion path is a trap — you win a small segment and get stuck there. Step 2's "expansion leverage" score is the most important filter.
- The wedge assumes the pain is confirmed. Don't narrow the entry point before you've validated that the root assumption ("is the pain real") is true — you might narrow into a non-problem.
- Some markets have network effects that require broad, simultaneous adoption (marketplaces, platforms). Wedge narrowing may not work when density requires critical mass across many segments at once.

## Connection to the Loop

Use at **thesis_mutation** when early evidence shows the market is real but adoption is scattered — you're not winning anyone decisively. Also useful at **ideation** when a large-market idea feels overwhelming: run through the three-dimension score early to find the natural entry point before building anything.
