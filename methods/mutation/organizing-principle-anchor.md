## Core Insight

A pivot is dangerous because it can scatter you into an unrelated space where none of your accumulated learning transfers. An organizing principle — a fixed statement of the *human need* you exist to serve, one level of abstraction above any product — solves this by constraining the pivot space to variations that still serve that need. The mechanism: hold the need constant and let the product be the variable. Every pivot then compounds prior learning (about the same users, the same job) instead of resetting it. The principle also doubles as a kill-test: if a product isn't serving the principle, that's the signal to pivot.

## Process

1. Articulate the organizing principle: the enduring human need, stated independently of any product or technology ("bringing people together, in the most human way possible, when they are physically apart").
2. Test each current product against it: is this actually serving the principle? (Rubin: Meerkat users couldn't engage/connect inside the app → it wasn't fulfilling the principle → pivot.)
3. Generate pivot options that all serve the same principle — vary the product, hold the need constant.
4. Prefer the pivot that both serves the principle and escapes the structural trap that killed the last version (Meerkat's trap: Twitter API dependence + broadcaster dynamics Twitter/FB would dominate).
5. Keep the principle fixed across multiple pivots; change it only with strong evidence that the need itself was wrong.

## Example

Ben Rubin's principle was "bringing people together when they are physically apart." Products pivoted repeatedly — AIR/Yevvo (livestream) → Meerkat (one-click broadcast, 2M users, killed when Twitter cut API access) → Houseparty (group video, 1M DAU, $52M round, sold to Epic, then #1 social app in 82 countries during COVID). "Going back to our organizing principle… enabled us to keep pivoting on our core idea."

## Limitations

- A principle that's too broad ("make people happy") constrains nothing and rationalizes any pivot — it must name a specific need.
- Held too rigidly, it can keep you in a doomed space when the honest move is to abandon the need entirely.
- It guides the *direction* of a pivot, not whether to pivot — pair it with evidence that the current product is failing.

## Connection to the Loop

A thesis-mutation method — the one to reach for when evidence says the current thesis is failing and you must reframe. It tells you which mutations preserve your accumulated evidence (same need, new product) versus which throw it away. Define the organizing principle early (at ideation) so it's ready when you need to mutate.
