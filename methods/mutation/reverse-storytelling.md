## Core Insight

Founders default to designing "page one" (the first product) and hoping it leads somewhere. Reverse storytelling inverts this: define the endgame first (the final chapter — what the company is "many years from now"), then work backwards to derive the *minimum* page-one wedge that credibly leads there. The mechanism: starting from the destination disciplines the wedge — you keep only entry points that actually connect to the vision, and you correctly reframe page one's job as *not* delivering the vision but merely earning the right to write chapter two (engagement/retention that buys time to build the later chapters).

## Process

1. Write the last chapter: what is this company at full scale? (Kaufman: "the everything store for digital services — Amazon for services.")
2. List the heavy machinery that only exists in late chapters (Fiverr ch.6: tens of thousands of categories, matching algorithms, deep search, metadata, support).
3. Remove those late-chapter "heroes and villains" and work backward toward page one.
4. Define page one by its real job: not to be the vision, but to get people to engage and stay long enough to read chapter two. Ask: "what point of engagement buys time to keep engaging and retaining this audience?"
5. Choose the wedge that is both minimal and on the path — a simplification that starts the story, not a feature that detours from it (Fiverr's flat $5 single price point).

## Example

Micha Kaufman knew Fiverr's last chapter ("Amazon for services") and wrote page one backwards: a dead-simple, single-price-point ($5) marketplace whose only job was to get people to engage and return — deferring categories, matching algorithms, and search to later chapters. "The sole purpose of page number one is for people to read it and get prepared… to be patient to read your next chapters."

## Limitations

- The endgame you write may be wrong; over-committing to a distant vision can make you force a wedge that fits the story but not the market.
- It optimizes wedge-to-vision fit, not wedge desirability — a page one that's on-path but that nobody wants still fails (pair with the Well Test).
- Works best when there's a plausible single destination; less useful for genuinely exploratory ideas whose endgame is unknowable.

## Connection to the Loop

An ideation method for choosing the wedge, and a mutation method for re-sequencing when you've entered at the wrong point. It's the top-down complement to the Path-Out Test: Path-Out starts from the beachhead and asks "is there a way out?"; Reverse Storytelling starts from the destination and asks "what beachhead leads here?" Run them together to pressure-test both ends of the path.
