## Core Insight

When a startup validates its initial wedge, it faces a structural question: where does defensible value actually live in this market? The answer is almost never where you started. Stack climbing is the deliberate move up, down, or across the value chain once a customer relationship is established — toward the position where the most value is captured and where switching costs are highest.

The mechanism: the initial product is a foot in the door that gives you customer trust and data. From that position, you can see which adjacent steps the customer needs, and which of those steps you're uniquely positioned to own. AWS started as internal infrastructure and externalized it. Stripe started with payment processing and climbed to Atlas (company formation), then Capital, then Tax. Shopify started with storefronts and climbed to fulfillment, payments, and financing. Each move was only possible because the prior relationship created the trust and the insight.

Stack climbing is the mutation you make when: "we've validated the wedge, customers are happy, but the business isn't big enough from here."

## Process

1. Map the full value chain your customer operates in — from their upstream inputs to their downstream outcomes.
2. Mark where you currently sit in the chain and what value you provide.
3. For each adjacent step (one up, one down, one sideways), ask:
   - Does the customer trust you enough to let you into this step?
   - Do you have data or insight from your current position that makes you better at this step than a cold entrant?
   - Is the value captured at this step larger than where you currently sit?
   - Would owning this step increase your switching costs?
4. Identify the highest-scoring adjacent step. Check that you could reach it without destroying trust in your current position.
5. Design the move: is it a new feature, an acquisition, a new product line, or a separate motion?

## Example

**Stripe**: Started with payment processing (developers → API → card charges). Adjacent steps were: entity formation (Atlas), capital (Stripe Capital), tax compliance (Stripe Tax), fraud prevention (Radar), banking (Treasury). Each step used the trust and payment data from the prior position.

**Shopify**: Started with storefronts. Climbed to payments (Shopify Payments), then fulfillment (Shopify Fulfillment Network), then capital (Shopify Capital), then B2B commerce. The trust built by hosting the merchant's core business made each climb possible.

**Veeva Systems**: Started with CRM for pharma sales reps. Climbed to clinical (Vault), then regulatory, then quality — owning more of the pharma value chain with each step, justified by compliance expertise and data continuity.

## Limitations

- Stack climbing only works once the wedge is genuinely validated. Climbing before the first step is stable means spreading resources across two unproven moves simultaneously.
- Not every adjacent step is accessible — moving up the stack can threaten existing partners or customers who were previously complementary (see: Amazon building its own fulfillment after being a marketplace).
- The most valuable position isn't always reachable. If the adjacent step requires resources, trust, or capabilities you can't acquire, the move is blocked.
- Horizontal moves (across to adjacent markets) often destroy focus without the defensibility benefits of vertical moves (up/down the stack).
