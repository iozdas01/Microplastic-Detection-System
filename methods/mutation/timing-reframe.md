## Core Insight

Many ideas that fail aren't wrong — they're early. The thesis is correct but the enabling conditions aren't yet in place: the cost curve hasn't dropped, the regulation hasn't passed, the adjacent behavior (smartphone adoption, cloud infrastructure, broadband) hasn't hit critical mass. When evidence says "the pain is real but adoption isn't happening," the question to ask before pivoting the idea is: are we wrong about the problem, or wrong about the timing?

Timing Reframe is the mutation you apply when evidence is directionally supportive but weak — customers agree the pain exists but aren't changing behavior. It separates "bad idea" from "right idea too early" by making the enabling conditions explicit and measurable. The output is either a survivable near-term product (designed to exist until conditions ripen) or a clear decision to wait.

## Process

1. Write down the specific thing your thesis requires to be true about the world right now:
   - A cost: "inference cost must be low enough that our unit economics work"
   - A behavior: "customers must already be comfortable with remote monitoring of physical assets"
   - A regulation: "insurance companies must be willing to cover AI-assisted inspections"
   - A technology: "sensors must be accurate enough at this range"
2. For each condition: is it true today? If not, what would make it true, and by when?
3. Identify the leading indicator for each: what's the earliest observable signal that the condition is approaching?
4. Decide between three options:
   - **Build for now**: is there a version that works under current conditions, even if smaller?
   - **Wait and watch**: monitor the leading indicators; don't build until they flip
   - **Enable the condition**: could you be the one to move the enabling condition? (risky but occasionally correct)
5. If building for now: rewrite the thesis around what's achievable in current conditions, and design an explicit "upgrade path" when conditions ripen.

## Example

**Electric vehicles (Tesla early)**: The thesis was correct (electric vehicles will replace combustion). The binding constraint was battery cost ($/kWh). Tesla's Timing Reframe: start with a premium vehicle where the cost premium is acceptable to early adopters, use that revenue to drive down the cost curve toward mass market. The roadster wasn't the vision — it was the survivable near-term product while the enabling condition (battery cost) improved.

**Too-early online grocery (Webvan, 1999–2001)**: The pain was real. The enabling conditions weren't: smartphone penetration, dense on-demand logistics, and consumer habit were all insufficient. The players who ran Timing Reframe correctly either waited, or narrowed to segments where conditions were closer — and the category was won a decade later (Instacart, DoorDash) once those conditions flipped.

## Limitations

- "It's just too early" is also the most common rationalization for a bad idea. Timing Reframe is only credible when you can name the specific enabling condition and its leading indicator. Vague claims ("the market isn't ready") aren't a reframe — they're a dodge.
- Some conditions don't ripen on a predictable schedule (regulation, culture). Be explicit about which conditions are trackable vs. speculative.
- Survivable near-term products can become traps if they consume all your resources and distract from the moment when conditions actually ripen.

## Connection to the Loop

Use at **thesis_mutation** when validation evidence is directionally positive but adoption is stalled. The method helps you distinguish "wrong thesis" from "right thesis, wrong timing" — two situations that look identical from the outside but require completely different responses. Also useful at **validation** as a diagnostic: before concluding an assumption is false, check whether timing is the actual variable.
