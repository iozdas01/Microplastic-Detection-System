## Core Insight

In B2B, verbal enthusiasm is the cheapest signal a buyer can give you. An LOI — a non-binding document where a company states conditional purchase intent — is three orders of magnitude more expensive for them to produce. It requires internal alignment, a named budget holder, a named price, and a named feature set. None of those things happen unless the buyer actually sees the pain as real and your solution as credible. The non-binding nature is not a weakness — it is what makes the LOI obtainable before you have a product. The binding comes later; the signal comes now.

The mechanism: LOIs collapse the "polite interest" problem. A buyer who would never stop a meeting but would also never buy will not sign an LOI. The act of drafting and signing forces the buyer to answer the question they were deferring: "Is this real enough that I would commit budget to it, conditionally?" That forced answer is the validation signal. Three to five LOIs from unaffiliated buyers in the same segment is "bankable validation" — the language investors and the founding team can use to confirm the buyer assumption before building.

## Process

1. Run 10–15 Mom Test interviews first. Do not request an LOI until you understand the buyer's pain in their language and can articulate the outcome they need.
2. Near the end of an interview where the buyer has escalated (described real past spend, introduced you to peers, asked when you'll be ready), ask: "Would you be willing to put something in writing that says if we build X by Y date, you'd plan to purchase?"
3. Draft a 1-page LOI with four elements: (a) the specific features or outcomes promised, (b) the delivery date, (c) the number of licenses or contract value, (d) the buyer's name and title. Keep it simple — elaborate LOIs die in legal review.
4. The buyer's willingness to circulate it internally is itself a signal — if they say "I need to check with procurement," you have a budget holder. If they say "I'll just sign it myself," you either have a champion without budget or a very small deal.
5. Target 3–5 LOIs from companies that don't know each other. One LOI from a warm contact is noise; three from cold contacts in the same segment is pattern.
6. Use the LOIs as the kill criterion for proceeding to build: if you cannot get 3 LOIs with a named price, the buyer assumption is unconfirmed.

## Example

Enterprise SaaS companies routinely use LOIs to derisk pre-seed raises. Glean (enterprise search) secured enterprise LOIs before launching publicly — those LOIs allowed them to price confidently and scope the MVP to exactly what the letter specified, not what they guessed the market wanted. The LOI eliminates scope creep at the source by making the buyer's requirements explicit before the first line of code is written.

## Limitations

- LOIs are non-binding. A buyer who signs one can and sometimes will walk away. Three to five LOIs are strong signal but not committed revenue.
- Works only in B2B. Consumer buyers do not sign LOIs; use Pre-Sale WTP Test instead.
- The LOI request can kill a warm conversation if timed wrong. Do not ask before the buyer has described real pain and expressed unsolicited interest in your solution.
- LOIs from the same organization (e.g., two divisions of the same company under the same buying committee) count as one data point, not two.
- Does not validate pricing — a buyer can sign an LOI at a price that later becomes a sticking point when the invoice arrives. Treat the LOI price as a directional anchor, not a committed price.

## Connection to the Loop

The primary hard-validation method for the **buyer assumption** in B2B contexts. Run after 10–15 Mom Test interviews have confirmed the pain assumption. The LOI closes the gap between "they said they'd buy" and "they've committed something to paper." Three to five LOIs is the kill criterion before proceeding to build. LOI content (features, price, date) directly scopes the first product version — the document is both a validation artifact and a product brief.
