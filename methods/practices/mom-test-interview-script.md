## Core Insight

Most founder interviews produce false positives because the founder mentions their idea — at which point the interview becomes a social interaction, not a data collection exercise. People are kind. They will validate your idea to avoid hurting your feelings. The Mom Test is named for the observation that even your mom, who loves you and wants you to fail at nothing, would give you accurate data if you ask her only about *her life* and *her behavior* — never about your idea.

The mechanism: behavior reveals truth; opinions lie. When you ask someone "do you think people would pay for X?" you get a social answer. When you ask "tell me about the last time you tried to solve X — what did you actually do? how much did you spend?" you get behavioral data. The pivot from opinion-gathering to behavior-mining is the entire method. The secondary mechanism: prior effort reveals real pain. If a buyer has never tried to solve the problem — no workaround, no budget spent, no time lost — the problem is a complaint, not a pain. Complaints don't convert.

## Process

1. **Open with a past behavior prompt, never with your idea:** "Tell me about the last time you [faced problem X]." This anchors the conversation in the real world, not a hypothetical.
2. **Dig for money:** "How much did you pay to solve it last time?" — or if no paid solution, "What did it cost you in time / resources / headcount?" Prior spend is the strongest signal that magnitude is real.
3. **Dig for effort:** "What workarounds have you hacked together?" — Prior effort (even crude DIY) confirms the buyer saw the problem as worth solving. No workaround = complaint, not pain.
4. **Listen for unsolicited escalation:** Does the buyer start introducing *you* to other people, asking when you'll have a product, or describing the problem in escalating terms without prompting? That is pull — the rarest and strongest signal.
5. **Force a commitment before closing:** Ask for an introduction, a follow-up call with a colleague, or a pilot agreement. If they hedged on every answer but enthusiastically agree to connect you to three peers, their hesitation was politeness. If they liked everything you said but won't make an intro, the interview was a social event.
6. **After the call:** Score the interview: (a) did they describe real past spend or effort? (b) did they escalate unprompted? (c) did they commit to something concrete? Any "no" is a signal to weight.

## Example

Buffer: Joel Gascoigne tweeted a two-sentence description of the product concept and linked to a "Plans and Pricing" page. People who clicked a plan were told "we're not ready yet — leave your email." He was not asking "would you pay?" — he was observing whether they *tried* to pay. That behavioral signal (attempted click-through to payment) replaced 500 biased opinion interviews. The insight maps directly to the Mom Test principle: behavior over opinion.

## Limitations

- Works only in pre-product or very early stages — once you have a product, usage data supersedes interviews
- The 3-question script is a floor, not a ceiling. Real interviews go 30–45 minutes. This script prevents the worst failure mode (idea-first) but does not replace curiosity
- Does not validate willingness to pay at a specific price point — only that prior spend existed. Pair with Pre-Sale WTP Test for price validation
- Can produce a false negative: a buyer who has never tried to solve the problem might still be an early adopter if the problem is truly new (no prior solution existed). Use judgment — absence of workarounds in a genuinely new category is less damning than absence in a category where solutions exist

## Connection to the Loop

The primary method for testing the **pain assumption** and **buyer assumption** through primary research. Run after Community Hangout Mining has surfaced the buyer vocabulary — use their language, not yours, in the opening prompt. Run before any solution or pricing experiments. The output (did they spend money? did they build workarounds? did they pull?) directly feeds the Evidence Ledger with behavioral data. 15–20 interviews until patterns repeat; if no pattern after 20, the target segment is too broad.
