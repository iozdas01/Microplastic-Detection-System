## Core Insight

Founders unconsciously filter out ideas before they consciously evaluate them. Two filters do most of the damage:

**Schlep filter**: "This problem is tedious, messy, or requires getting involved with the real world in unpleasant ways." Thousands of programmers knew payments were painful before Stripe. None of them built Stripe because they unconsciously recoiled from dealing with banks, fraud, and financial regulation. The idea was invisible — not because it was hard to see, but because it was unpleasant to look at.

**Unsexy filter**: "This domain is boring or beneath me — I'm not interested in it." PG built Viaweb (online store software) despite not caring about e-commerce. The problem needed solving; the domain didn't need to be interesting.

The insight: the best ideas often look unappealing precisely because they're hard enough that no one wants to deal with them — which means there's no competition. The discomfort is a moat.

## Process

Run this as a self-audit when you feel like you've explored a space and found nothing:

1. List the problem spaces you've dismissed or mentally skipped over
2. For each, identify which filter fired:
   - **Schlep**: "This is too messy / too much real-world complexity / requires dealing with X"
   - **Unsexy**: "This domain is boring / not something I'd want to spend time on"
   - **Neither**: genuinely dismissed for a substantive reason (no pain, too small)
3. For everything filtered by schlep or unsexy: is the filter actually about the quality of the idea, or about your preferences?
4. Stress-test the schlep cases especially — what makes them messy? Is that messiness a structural barrier to others, which means it's actually a moat?

## Example

**Stripe**: payments were clearly painful. The schlep was dealing with banks, KYC, fraud systems, and financial compliance. That schlep was the reason no one had built it — which meant first-mover advantage for the team willing to do it.

**Viaweb (became Yahoo Stores)**: e-commerce was "unsexy" to PG as a programmer. He could see the technical problem was real and unsolved. He built it anyway. Acquired by Yahoo.

## Limitations

- Not all schlep is opportunity — some problems are messy because they're genuinely unsolvable at this stage (legal, regulatory, physical constraints)
- Unsexy problems require founder motivation to be solved well — building something you despise for years has real costs
- The audit surfaces candidate ideas, not validated ones — still needs the full assumption-testing loop

## Connection to the Loop

Use at the **ideation** stage specifically when you feel like you've looked at a space and found nothing. The filter audit is how you check whether "nothing there" is a real conclusion or a psychological artifact. Run it before concluding an idea space is empty.

## Note: two additional filters (Jared Friedman, Startup School 2020)

Friedman frames the same phenomenon as unconscious biases that "kill good ideas before they surface" and adds two filters beyond schlep and unsexy — audit for these too:

- **Ambition filter**: dismissing an idea because it seems *too* ambitious. Big, intimidating ideas get filtered out before evaluation, but the scale is often the reason there's no competition.
- **Competitor filter**: avoiding a space because competitors already exist. Existing competitors are frequently a sign the market is real, not a reason to stay out — Dropbox launched into a crowded file-sync space and won. When you catch yourself skipping a space "because someone already does it," that's the competitor filter firing.

Run all four filters (schlep, unsexy/boring, ambition, competitor) as the self-audit checklist.
