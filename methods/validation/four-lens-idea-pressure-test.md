## Core Insight

The strongest consumer products satisfy two *different* needs at once, and most founders only name one. Pressure-testing an idea against four lenses forces the others into the open:

1. **Functional need** — the concrete task the product does. This is why people *try* it.
2. **Emotional need** — status, relief, delight, belonging. This is why people *tell others*, which unlocks the word-of-mouth growth loop.
3. **Billion-plus market** — large today or plausibly becoming large. This sets the ceiling on the outcome and shapes fundraising and acquisition interest.
4. **Breakthrough UX** — something that feels a little like magic. Not strictly required, but a powerful multiplier on trial and retention.

The mechanism is that each lens explains a different part of the growth engine: functional need explains *trial*, emotional need explains *referral*, market explains the *ceiling*, and UX explains *pull*. A product strong on all four has a built-in engine rather than a feature people merely tolerate. The exercise's real value is that it makes you name whether each need is *acute* or just a nice-to-have — and the weakest of the four is your riskiest assumption.

## Process

1. State the idea in a single sentence.
2. **Functional:** what concrete task does it accomplish, and is that need acute or nice-to-have?
3. **Emotional:** what emotional need does it meet, and would using it make someone want to tell a friend? (If not, there's no organic loop.)
4. **Market:** is it a $1B+ market, or one that could become large? Name what must be true for it to get big.
5. **UX:** is there something novel that feels like magic when you use it?
6. Rate each lens honestly. Functional + emotional + market is the recipe; UX is the multiplier. Whichever lens is weakest is the assumption to test first.

## Example

Instagram at launch scores 4-for-4. Functional: take a good-looking photo fast on a phone camera that produced mediocre images. Emotional: identity and status — a curated, beautiful version of your life, plus the small delight of filters — which drove intense word of mouth. Market: effectively everyone with a smartphone. UX: one-tap filters felt like magic in 2010. The double need (functional *and* emotional) is exactly why it spread rather than sitting as a niche photo tool.

## Limitations

- It's a screen, not evidence — a paper 4-for-4 still needs its assumptions tested, especially market size and why-now.
- "Emotional need" and "breakthrough UX" are the easiest to rationalize after the fact; be ruthlessly honest about whether they're real or wished-for.
- Weighted toward consumer products. Many enterprise ideas win on functional value + ROI without an emotional/word-of-mouth loop, so a low emotional score isn't always fatal there.
- Silent on timing, distribution feasibility, and founder-market fit — those still need separate treatment.

## Connection to the Loop

Use at **ideation / assumption extraction** as a fast screen before committing to an idea. It converts "I have a problem to solve" into four named, testable assumptions — functional pain, emotional/distribution loop, market size, and UX differentiation — and surfaces which one is weakest. That weakest lens becomes the riskiest assumption you carry into prioritization and testing.

**Relationship to [Idea Quality Score](../validation/idea-quality-score.md).** Both score an idea on four criteria, but they answer different questions and are complementary. Idea Quality Score (Caldwell/Friedman) triages *which candidate idea* to pursue using founder/market fit, pain certainty, novel insight, and market size — it's about you and your edge. The Four-Lens test asks whether *this idea is a great product* with a built-in growth engine: functional need (trial) + emotional need (word of mouth) + market + UX. Run Idea Quality Score to pick the idea; run Four-Lens to pressure-test the one you picked.
