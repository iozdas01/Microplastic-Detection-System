## Core Insight

What customers *say* they want is usually a surface proxy for an underlying driver they can't articulate — often a fear or a constraint. The mechanism: people describe their desire in terms of the solutions they already know (reputation, reviews, social proof), but the real job is the thing underneath (eliminate downside risk). Build to the stated want and you build a commodity; decode the latent need and you can solve it in a form the customer would never have requested — and that form is often the actual innovation. Customers can validate a need; they cannot design the solution.

## Process

1. Collect the stated wants verbatim ("reputable school, reviews, social proof, be in the news").
2. Ask what each stated want is *for* — keep going until you hit a driver they can't reduce further (want reputation → because they fear it won't work and they'll be on the hook → the real need is to not bear the downside risk).
3. Name the latent need in terms of the driver, not the requested solution ("let them take the risk without suffering the downside").
4. Design for the latent need even if it takes a form no customer asked for (Lambda's Income Share Agreement — "students would never have told us to use an ISA; they didn't know what it was").
5. Validate that solving the latent need unlocks behavior the stated-want solution didn't ("then everything starts to work").

## Example

Lambda School students all said they wanted a reputable, well-reviewed school. Digging past that, the real driver was risk aversion — "they thought their problem was lack of money, but the problem was actually risk." So Lambda built the Income Share Agreement (pay only after you get a job) — a solution students would never have named, addressing the latent need directly.

## Limitations

- Decoding latent needs shades easily into projecting your own theory onto customers — the discipline is that the latent-need solution must actually change behavior, or you've just rationalized what you wanted to build (the "faster horse" trap cuts both ways).
- It's slower and higher-judgment than taking stated wants at face value, and it's wrong when there genuinely is no deeper need than the stated one.
- Distinct from Five Whys: Five Whys traces a *problem* to its root cause; this traces a stated *desire* to its underlying driver and outputs a solution the customer wouldn't request.

## Connection to the Loop

A validation method for the pain and buyer assumptions, and an ideation source for non-obvious solutions. Use it whenever customer research produces a clear stated want — before building to that want, run one more layer to check it isn't a proxy. Pairs with Five Whys (problem side) and Use-Case Multiplication (breadth side).
