## Core Insight

The most important early question is "am I the only one with this problem?" The sharp validation signal isn't polite agreement — it's when you describe the solution and the listener *spontaneously generates use cases you never imagined*. That generativity proves two things at once: the pain is real enough that people map it onto their own situation unprompted, and the market is broad because the use cases multiply beyond your own. Nodding is cheap; unprompted use-case generation is expensive and honest.

## Process

1. Describe the solution (not the problem) to a target user, concretely enough that they can picture using it.
2. Ask the deliberately open question: "Would you have a use for this?"
3. Stay silent and watch what they do. Polite "sounds useful" = weak. Strong signal: they pause, then say "wait — could I use it for [X you never mentioned]?"
4. Count and log the unprompted use cases across conversations. Breadth of spontaneous use cases = breadth of the market.
5. Only claim validation once enough people have independently generated their own use cases that you're clearly not the only one ("after having that conversation enough times").

## Example

Jeff Lawson pitched a "telephone API" to developers. They'd scratch their heads, then generate their own: "could I notify my customers when a package ships so they stop calling support?" That spontaneous use-case generation — repeated across many devs — gave the conviction to start Twilio (now 10M+ developers, 200k+ business customers).

## Limitations

- Imagining a use is weaker than paying — a spontaneous use case ≈ 2-3 on the evidence hierarchy; payment = 5. Enthusiasm can evaporate at the pricing conversation.
- Works best for horizontal / developer tools where users can self-map; weaker for products requiring behavior change the user can't yet visualize (see Stated Want vs. Latent Need).

## Connection to the Loop

A validation method for the pain (A2) and market-breadth assumptions. Pairs with the Well Test: the Well Test measures depth of one user's need; Use-Case Multiplication measures breadth across users. Run both.
