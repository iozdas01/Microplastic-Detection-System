## Core Insight

Obsession is a filter for good vs. great. The mechanism: building a company is a multi-year endurance test, and the founders who survive it are the ones who would work on the problem anyway — for free, in their spare time, losing track of time. Obsession is a proxy for that endurance, and it usually rides on top of a real information advantage (you obsess about things you understand deeply). Persistence is the temporal version of the same signal: an idea you keep rejecting on paper ("who am I to do this?") but that won't leave you over months is registering conviction your rational mind hasn't caught up to. Both are self-tests for the founder-market-fit assumption (A3).

## Process

1. State the idea, then check your own reaction rather than the market's: would you work on this in your free time, unpaid?
2. Time test: when working on it, do you lose track of time? Is there something you'd rather be doing?
3. Persistence test: has this idea kept returning after you rejected it, and for how long? (Chandra: 6-9 months of "no.")
4. Separate obsession-with-the-problem from obsession-with-being-a-founder — only the former survives pivots.
5. If the honest answer is "good idea, but I could take it or leave it," treat A3 as failing regardless of how attractive the market looks.

## Example

Manish Chandra rejected the collaborative-shopping idea for 6-9 months — "who am I to do this? I'm an enterprise software guy" — but "the idea just wouldn't go away." It became Kaboodle, then Poshmark. NFX's litmus: "Don't start a company unless you can't not do it… your brain is exploding with the idea."

## Limitations

- Obsession validates A3 (founder-market-fit), not A2 (real, expensive pain) — plenty of obsessed founders build things nobody wants.
- It can be self-deceptive: sunk-cost obsession, or ego-obsession with being a founder rather than with the problem.
- It says nothing about market size or timing. Use it to decide which idea *you* should pursue, not whether the idea is good.

## Connection to the Loop

The test for the mandatory founder-market-fit assumption (A3). Run it at ideation when choosing among candidate ideas, and revisit it whenever a pivot is on the table — if the pivot kills your obsession, the endurance advantage is gone.
