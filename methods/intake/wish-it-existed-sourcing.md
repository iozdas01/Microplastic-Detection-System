## Core Insight

Build something you personally wish existed. The mechanism that makes this powerful: you are a *validated user*. Pain certainty is maximal because you feel the problem yourself; you can dogfood the product continuously; and you know instinctively when a crappy v1 is actually good enough, because you're the one trying to use it. Friedman lists "you're making something you personally want" as one of the top signs an idea is probably good — precisely because it collapses the distance between founder and customer.

This is distinct from previous-job need mining (which sources pain from your work environment) — this sources pain from your own life as a consumer or user. The classic case: DoorDash's founders couldn't get Thai food delivered in the suburbs, so they built the thing they wanted. The want was real, personal, and recurring.

The one hazard is representativeness: your want is validated for *you*, but not yet for a market. The whole risk collapses onto the market assumption — how many other people share the want.

## Process

1. List things you've genuinely wished existed or been frustrated you couldn't buy or use.
2. Filter for the *recurring* wants, not one-off annoyances — a want you feel repeatedly is a stronger signal.
3. Honestly assess whether you're a representative user or an outlier with idiosyncratic taste.
4. Estimate how many people share the want, and how intensely — this is the assumption most in doubt.
5. Build a crappy v1 for yourself first and use it. If it fails the "would I actually use this daily" test on you, it fails.

## Example

**DoorDash**: the founders, living in the suburbs, couldn't get their favorite Thai food delivered. They built the delivery service they personally wanted. Because they were the target user, they knew exactly what "good enough" looked like and could validate every iteration against their own experience.

## Limitations

- You may be an outlier — a want you feel intensely may be niche. The market assumption is entirely unvalidated by your own desire.
- Personal want does not equal willingness to pay at scale; you'd use it for free, but would strangers pay?
- Consumer wants in particular can feel universal to you and be tiny in reality.
- Being your own customer can also blind you to how differently others use the product — dogfooding validates your use case, not everyone's.

## Connection to the Loop

Use at **ideation** to source an idea seed with a pre-validated pain assumption (you). Feed it straight into **assumption extraction**: your own experience is the pain assumption (strong), and your estimate of how many others share the want becomes the market assumption (weak, untested). Prioritize testing the market assumption first, since that's where all the risk now sits.
