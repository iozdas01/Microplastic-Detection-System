## Core Insight

In any job, people regularly encounter problems that no existing tool solves. They say "why doesn't someone make x? If someone made x, we'd buy it in a second." These moments are highly validated: you know the pain is real (you lived it), you know it's buildable in principle (you know the domain), and the fact that nothing was built is a market signal — not a sign the problem doesn't matter, but a sign no one has solved it yet.

PG: "One good trick is to ask yourself whether in your previous job you ever found yourself saying 'Why doesn't someone make x? If someone made x we'd buy it in a second.'"

This is distinct from ideation exercises: you're mining verified pain from your own experience, not generating hypothetical problems. The demand is pre-validated because you would have paid for it yourself.

## Process

1. Think back through every job or role you've had — what were the recurring frustrations?
2. Specifically recall moments where you or colleagues said "why doesn't this exist?" or had to build a workaround
3. For each: what exactly was missing? What did you have to do instead?
4. Filter for the ones where demand was universal in your environment — not just your preference, but something everyone on the team felt
5. Apply the Well Test: would a crappy v1 have been used immediately? How urgently was it needed?
6. Check: is your expertise in that domain an asset (you can evaluate quality, talk to buyers) or a liability (you've normalized the problem and can't see it clearly)?

## Example

Hotmail began as something its founders wrote to communicate about their *previous* startup idea while working day jobs. The tool they needed — private web-based email not controlled by their employer — was obvious once they needed it and didn't exist.

Rajat Suri of E la Carte decided to build software for restaurants and got a job as a waiter first — to ensure he was mining real needs from inside the domain, not projecting from outside.

## Limitations

- Your previous job may be a niche with no broader market — validate whether the need is specific to your employer/industry or universal
- You may be the only person frustrated by something that others have learned to accept — check if colleagues shared the frustration or just you
- Domain expertise can make you better at evaluating solutions but worse at seeing the problem clearly (you've adapted to the workaround)
- The "I would have paid for this" test is weaker than "I actually paid for the closest thing available" — be honest about the difference

## Connection to the Loop

Use this at **ideation** to source the idea seed, and immediately feed the result into **assumption extraction**: the specific memory of the need becomes the pain assumption, and your estimate of universality becomes the market assumption. Both are now concrete rather than hypothetical.

## Note: in-house build productization (Jared Friedman, Startup School 2020)

Friedman adds a sharper variant of expertise mining: instead of only recalling unmet needs, ask **"what did the company build in-house that others might need?"** When a company builds an internal tool to solve its own problem, that's a pre-validated product — the pain was real enough to justify building, and the same need likely exists at other companies. This is a stronger signal than "why doesn't someone make x," because the solution already exists and works internally; the opportunity is to productize it. Examples: SnapDocs, Lattice, and Mixpanel all trace to needs founders saw from inside prior companies. When mining a previous job, list both the unmet needs *and* the internal tools the company built that weren't available to buy.

**Repetition as revealed preference (Lloyd Tabb / Elad Gil, First Round Review).** The signal is strongest when you've built the same tool *more than once, across different jobs or companies*. Paying the build cost repeatedly is hard evidence the need is durable and unserved — and it means you already have deep founder-market fit. Lloyd Tabb kept hand-building one-off data tools at multiple companies before that repetition told him to build Looker; engineers rebuilding the same on-call/alerting glue at company after company is the pattern behind PagerDuty. When mining, note not just *what* you rebuilt but *how many times* — recurrence across contexts is the tell.
