## Core Insight

Strong emotional reactions — frustration, outrage, delight, obsession — are signals that a problem has real human weight. Problems that make people want to "slam a door" are sticky in memory and sticky for customers. Founders who build from genuine emotion tend to have better instincts for user pain and higher intrinsic motivation when things get hard.

The mechanism: emotion is a compression of accumulated experience. When something makes you furious or lights you up, it's because you've encountered the friction or the delight many times, from many angles. That accumulated pattern is more valuable than a single observation.

**Limitation for our framework:** Emotional signals are personal, not universal. Your frustration ≠ market pain. This method identifies candidate problems worth investigating — it does not validate them. An emotional signal is the start of an assumption, not evidence for one.

## Process

1. Walk through your day noticing what frustrates, excites, or bothers you
2. Skim widely (headlines, podcast titles, forum topics) to increase exposure volume
3. Capture the reaction immediately — before you think about whether it's "startup worthy"
4. Note: what specifically triggered the reaction? (not just the topic — the specific friction point)
5. Over time, look for patterns: which signals keep recurring? Which topics make you "wave your hands"?

## Limitations

- Personal emotion ≠ market pain — this is a signal to investigate, not evidence
- Strong emotion about a problem doesn't mean you're the right person to solve it
- Can lead founders to build for themselves ("scratch your own itch") without checking if others share the itch
- Works best as an input to the ideation loop, not a substitute for assumption testing
- Less useful after you've already identified an idea — this is pre-ideation

## Note on placement in the loop

This method lives **before** the assumption loop starts — it's how you generate the idea seed. Once you have the seed, switch to assumption extraction and testing. Emotional signals don't substitute for evidence.
