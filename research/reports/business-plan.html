01

The proposition

American furniture manufacturing lost to imports because it is labour-heavy. US wood-furniture imports run $23.7bn a year, and the domestic industry that remains is tiny and manual — of 2,126 non-upholstered wood furniture plants in the country, 1,280 have fewer than five employees.

The usual answer is a large automated plant, which costs $3–5m and has to find buyers for 11,000 chairs a year on day one. We propose the opposite order: build the smallest cell that is genuinely unattended, sell into it, and let demand pull the capital in rather than the other way round.

$329,000 buys a machining cell that runs lights-out. One CNC, one tending robot, one person on days. It makes up to 9,966 chairs a year and breaks even at 771 — including a founder salary and paid customer acquisition.

The core economic fact

A chair sells for $633 and costs $149 in materials, packaging and freight. Everything else in this document is about whether we can find people to buy it — not about whether we can make it.

02

The product

A dining chair in white oak, configurable in dimension and finish, joined with kigumi — the Japanese timber joinery tradition that uses interlocking wooden joints and tapered keys instead of fasteners.

The chair is nine part types, 25 pieces, and 47 machined joint operations. There is no hardware line in the bill of materials because there is no hardware: no screws, no cam locks, no dowels, no glue. The parts hold each other.

Why the joinery decides the whole business

This is not a styling choice. Three consequences fall out of it, and together they are the reason a two-person company can compete with a factory:

  • There is no assembly line. Not an automated one — none at all. A conventional furniture plant spends most of its labour and most of its floor on assembly. We deleted that station by designing the product so the customer performs it, in their kitchen, with their hands.
  • It ships flat and it ships cheap. An assembled chair is roughly six times the shipping volume of its parts. Freight is $28 a chair instead of $124.
  • Configuration is nearly free. The joints are parametric. Changing a seat height or a back angle changes numbers in a CAD model that regenerates the toolpaths. There is no retooling, so a one-off costs what a standard costs — which is the entire commercial argument.
The engineering fork — resolve before buying

The original design specifies 5-axis machining for the tsugite and shiguchi geometry. A 5-axis centre is $395,000; a 3-axis router with a rotary 4th axis is $90,000. This plan assumes the joints can be redesigned to cut on the cheaper machine. That is a $305,000 question and it has not been answered. Prototyping the joint set on a rented 4-axis router is the first thing we do, and it costs under $5,000.

What is automated, precisely

Stated exactly, because the difference between "autonomous cell" and "fully automated factory" is the difference between a claim that survives a site visit and one that doesn't.

Sell Auto

Configurator

Customer sets dimensions and finish, pays. The order lands as a parameter set, not an email.

Design Auto

Parametric CAD

Parameters drive the model, the model regenerates the toolpaths. No human draws anything.

Cut Auto

Lights-out CNC

The cobot loads blanks and unloads finished parts. Runs 13 hours a night with the building empty.

Finish Hands

Sand, oil, cure

Day shift. One person. Phase 2 automates this for $214k when volume justifies it.

Check Hands

Test-fit the joints

Every chair is assembled once and taken apart. The joint is the product; we do not ship one unverified.

Ship Hands

Flat-pack

Box, label, carrier pickup. Roughly six minutes a chair.

What the tending robot actually buys

Not labour. All the material handling in the entire plant is 12.9 minutes a chair — at 20 chairs a week that is 224 hours a year, or a tenth of one person. A robot bought to save that would take 45 years to pay back.

It is bought for machine hours. The same $90,000 CNC produces three times as much when a robot can keep feeding it after everyone has gone home:

$85,000 of robot does the work of $196,000 of additional machines, and adds no headcount. That is the strongest capital-efficiency line in the plan, and it is why the robot belongs in stage one rather than stage three.

What we are not buying, and why

The large plan carried $145,000 of MES, PLM and nesting licences. At 20 chairs a week there is nothing for a manufacturing execution system to execute: the production schedule is the Shopify order list, and the work-in-progress tracker is a shelf you can see from the door. We add an MES when we cannot see the whole plant at once — realistically past 100 chairs a week.

Break-even

194 orders a year — 15 chairs a week. That covers every fixed cost, a full founder salary and all customer acquisition. Gross profit per order is $1,935 against a $500 acquisition cost, so each order contributes $1,435.

The market, honestly

This section is deliberately unflattering. The manufacturing case is strong and the demand case is not yet proven, and an investor should know which is which before committing.

The one number that decides everything

Only 0.55% of people searching for dining furniture search for it custom. That is the market as it exists today, where custom means a long wait and a large premium from a joinery shop. Our thesis is that configurable furniture at close to stock price is a different offer to a much larger audience. We have no evidence for that. Depending on whether the true figure is 0.6% or 8%, this is a $5.9m market or a $175.7m one — a 30× range resting on a single untested assumption.

The saving grace is scale. At 15 chairs a week we break even, which is 0.036% of the $5.3bn dining category. Even the pessimistic floor is roughly ten times the revenue this cell can absorb. We do not need the assumption to be true to build a profitable business — we need it to be true to build a large one.

The competitive read points the same way. The industry is 1,280 sub-five-person shops, which is what makes automation viable and simultaneously means there is no concentrated share to take. We compete on price and lead time against local joiners, and on configurability against imports.

What stage one proves

Four gates. Each has a number attached, and the decision to spend the next several million depends on clearing all four.

JointCan kigumi joints be cut on a 4-axis router to a fit tolerance customers accept?50 test joints at <0.2mm, assembled by untrained peopleMonth 2, before machine purchase

The demand gate is the one worth building the company around. A working configurator with real prices, taking real money, run against paid traffic, is the only instrument that can measure the 0.55%-versus-8% question — and it is included in the $329,000.

The order matters. The joint gate closes in month two for under $5,000 and it is the one that can invalidate the machine choice. Nothing is bought until it passes.

How it is released

  1. $45,000 on signing. Joint prototyping, three equipment quotes, Keyword Planner, configurator design. Roughly ten weeks.
  2. $304,000 on the joint gate passing. Equipment ordered, lease signed, configurator built.
  3. $126,000 as working capital drawn against the operating plan.

If the joint gate fails, $430,000 is unspent and the decision to continue is taken with the answer in hand rather than the equipment already on the floor.

Still to agree

Valuation, equity split and governance are not proposed here — they are the conversation this document is meant to start. What is proposed is the shape: $475,000, staged against four technical gates, for a cell that can carry the business to $2m of revenue before anyone has to raise again.
