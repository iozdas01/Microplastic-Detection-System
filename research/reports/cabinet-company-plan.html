More than half the labour in a custom cabinet business happens before a single sheet is cut. That is why custom cabinets cost three times stock: you are not paying for better manufacturing, you are paying for a designer's afternoon.

Which means the bottleneck is a person at a desk

The machines idle at 60% while the designer is saturated. Buying robots for the shop floor would change nothing at all — you would be automating the station that already has 40% spare capacity.

What AI is worth here

Take design and engineering from 13.5 hours to 1.5 — AI drafts, a human approves — and the constraint moves to the machines at 228 kitchens a year. Revenue goes from $2.42m to $4.00m and EBITDA from $1.22m to $2.28m, with the same building, the same machines and the same three people. That is a $1.06m/year return on software.

The app is the company

This is worth being blunt about. Anyone can buy the same router for $150,000. Nobody else has a consumer app that takes somebody standing in their own kitchen holding a laser and returns a priced, engineered, machine-ready job. The factory is not the moat — it is what makes the app worth owning, because it is what lets us quote a price nobody routing the work through a designer can match.

It also explains why the build order in section 12 is software first. Phases 1 and 2 build this app and sell five kitchens through it with the manufacturing subcontracted. If customers will not configure their own kitchen, no amount of machinery fixes that — and it costs $120,000 to find out rather than $528,000.

Where a human still appears

Two places, both deliberate. One person reviews every design before it releases to the machine — that is the 1.5 hours in section 04, not zero. And the installer is a human being for two days at the customer’s house. The app removes the designer, not every person in the chain.

01

Intake and measurement

AI + human check

The customer measures their own kitchen with a shipped laser distance meter, guided step by step by the app: three heights per wall, both diagonals, floor level at five points, services and appliances. A phone scan and photos ride alongside as corroboration, not as the measurement. The app cross-checks every reading and returns a dimensioned room model with a confidence score on each span.

Replaces the site visit outright, at ~$140 a kitchen — see section 08.

02

Layout generation

AI

A constraint solver generates valid layouts — appliance clearances, the 15" landing space beside a range, filler strips, standard box widths, door swing, code. A language model handles the fuzzy half: turning "we bake a lot" and "a coffee station near the window" into storage allocation and placement. Three to five options, rendered.

Replaces 4–6 hours of designer time. The customer picks.

03

Quoting

Deterministic

Layout → box schedule → parts → materials → price. Pure arithmetic over the parametric model. This binds you to a contract, so it must be exactly reproducible and never generative.

Instant, versus days. This alone changes how the business sells.

04

Engineering and CAM

Deterministic

Box schedule → part list → nesting solver → G-code, edgebander program, hardware pick list, part labels, assembly sheets. Cabinet Vision, Microvellum and Mozaik already do exactly this; it is solved software, not an AI problem.

Replaces 4.5 hours of engineering. Simulated before it runs.

05

Scheduling and shop orchestration

AI

Which jobs batch onto which sheets, in what order, to minimise offcut waste and still hit promise dates. Re-nesting when a part fails QC. Reordering the queue when material is late. This is a genuine optimisation problem and it is where AI belongs on the shop floor.

Sheet yield is the second-largest variable cost after doors.

06

Customer and supplier agents

AI

Order status, change requests, install scheduling, purchase orders to the door supplier and the sheet distributor, chasing late material.

Replaces most of an operations coordinator.

The three problems that are actually hard

01

A layout a human would not redraw

The real AI problem

Clearances, the 15″ landing beside a range, work triangle, standard box widths, door swing, code — those are a constraint solver, and solvers are reliable. The hard half is taste and intent: turning “we bake a lot” and “coffee near the window” into storage allocation. A language model proposes; the solver filters to legal; the customer chooses. The measurable target is the share of generated layouts a designer accepts with no edit, tracked from day one.

02

Tolerance propagation

Deterministic, and unforgiving

Measurement uncertainty has to flow through to a decision: this run is inside its envelope and can cut, or it needs wider fillers, or it needs a human. Every run carries its own envelope derived from the wall's plumb and square deviation. Getting this wrong scraps a $17,550 kitchen, which is why the verdict is arithmetic and never a model output.

03

The post-processor

Deterministic, simulated, reviewed line by line

The one component where a wrong answer is a machine crash rather than a wasted sheet. Generated by agents like everything else, but reviewed by a person, versioned, and simulated before every single run. This is the line in section 07 that does not get crossed.

Why this is a two-person job and not a team of six

Three properties make it unusually suited to coding agents. The specification already exists — frameless cabinetry is the 32mm system, standardised and published, so it has to be encoded rather than invented. The tests are mechanical — does the box close, do parts sum to the carcass, does it nest, does the hardware clear — so agents iterate against ground truth instead of an opinion. And the blast radius is bounded: a bad generation produces a proposed cut list, and the cut list is simulated before it reaches a spindle.

What this costs, honestly

The capital plan carries $120,000 for this, which is the cash cost of building it founder-led with agents. It is not the replacement cost. Two to four engineer-years of domain-heavy work, including a safety-relevant post and a contractually binding quote engine, would be several times that at market rates. Both numbers are true and they answer different questions — what we spend, and what a competitor would have to spend to catch up.

07

The line not to cross

A language model must never emit G-code

A hallucinated Z-depth or feed rate on a 24,000 rpm spindle is a machine crash, a destroyed $2,000 vacuum table, or an injury. The same applies to any number that becomes a binding price.

The architecture that keeps this safe is one sentence:

AI writes the generator.
The generator writes the G‑code.

AI coding agents build and maintain the parametric cabinet library, the nesting integration, the quote engine and the front end. Those are ordinary software with precise specifications. What they produce is a deterministic program, and that program — reviewed by a human, versioned, tested, and simulated before every run — is what talks to the machine.

08

How autonomous can it actually be?

Asked directly, answered directly. Inside the building: almost entirely. Outside it, one blocker survives — installation. The other, measurement, turns out to be a design problem rather than a physical one.

One hard stop, not two

Measurement — solved by design, not by a visit

Cabinets need ±3mm, and iPhone LiDAR and RoomPlan deliver ±10–20mm. But that argues against the wrong instrument. A laser distance meter is spec’d at ±1.5–2.0mm over 10–30m, costs $50–150, and fits in a jiffy bag. The accuracy needed to cut a kitchen is already cheap enough to post to the customer.

What an untrained person lacks is not precision but protocol: knowing to measure each wall at three heights to catch out-of-plumb, take both diagonals to catch out-of-square, and check floor level at five points. That is an app problem, and the app can validate itself — if the two diagonals disagree by more than a threshold, the room is not square, and the design adapts rather than the customer being trusted.

The ready-to-assemble cabinet industry already sells this way. Customers submit their own measurements and photos, a layout comes back, boxes ship. The in-home visit is an optional add-on, not a gate. So the visit is not physically required — it is a risk choice, and one this industry has already made differently at scale.

The economics decide it, and they are lopsided

The honest answer, and why it does not matter much

A hardware-free kitchen is buildable if it is drawer-led below and tambour or open above. But look at what removing hinges is worth: all hardware is $470 of a $5,831 variable cost — 8%, and hinges are a fraction of that. Meanwhile soft-close is something custom buyers actively pay for. Designing it out removes a feature people want in order to save a rounding error.

The prize was never the hinge — it was the labour. The router already bores the 35mm cup in the same pass as the panel. Clip-on hinges snap onto a mounting plate in seconds with no tools, which means the door can be hung by the installer, or by the customer, without touching the 210-minute hardware station in our building. Be honest about where the labour went

Of the 10.5 hours removed, none was destroyed — it was relocated to the job site. The installer is already there for two days; snapping locking joints together adds roughly two hours to a visit that is happening anyway. That is still a real gain — no double-handling, no carrying assembled boxes up stairs, and roughly a quarter of the shipping volume — but it is not the same as eliminating the work, and an investor will ask.

Where a robot actually belongs — and where it does not

Two things get confused here. Box assembly is not a robotics problem in this plan, because it is deleted rather than automated — locking joints and flat-pack remove it. Building boxes in our building and shipping them assembled would also undo the freight saving, since an assembled carcass is several times the shipping volume of its parts.

The station that genuinely wants a robot is hardware insertion and kitting: hinges, slides and pulls, then bundling a kitchen's worth of varied parts into labelled packs. That is 210 minutes today and roughly 40 with the $55k auto hinge inserter already costed in phase 5.

On the new robot foundation models

Skild AI announced S1 on 25 August 2026: a model that learns a ten-minute task from a single human video, reporting 66% success on tasks it had never seen against 9% for a language-prompted policy, with kit assembly among the demonstrated tasks. The capability direction is pointed squarely at the part of our hardware station that fixed automation handles badly — varied parts, in varied poses, in a job mix that changes every kitchen.

Two things make it a later bet rather than a phase-1 one. 66% first-pass success is a demo number, not a production number — a station that fails one kitchen in three needs a person standing beside it, which is the cost the robot was bought to remove. And at announcement there were no weights, no API and no published paper, so there is nothing to integrate against or to price.

The posture that costs nothing: build the hardware cell with a swappable policy layer. Run conventional fixed automation now, keep the part presentation and fixturing generic, and adopt a foundation model when one ships with an integration path and a published success rate on a fixed part set. It is worth prototyping the moment weights exist — it is not worth planning capital around today.

Architect for all of it — buy it in the right order

Full in-plant autonomy costs $320,000 more — auto load/unload $75k, robot edgebander feed $95k, hinge inserter $55k, vision QC and labelling $35k, kitting cell $60k. At 1.8 hours a kitchen, two people could produce 2,142 kitchens a year. The machines cap at 228.

The automation would sit nine times idle — the identical mistake as a $4.78m plant built for demand that does not exist yet. Fix the front office first; it costs software, not steel, and it is the only constraint currently binding.

09

The manufacturing plan

Sheet in, finished cabinet out, nobody touching it. Eight stations, two robots and a case clamp — and one architectural fact that makes the whole thing tractable.

The fact the plan rests on

A cabinet carcass is topologically identical every time

Five or six panels, the same relationships, the same assembly order. Between a 300mm wall unit and a 1200mm base unit only the numbers change. That is not true of a chair, a table or almost any other furniture, and it is why robotic assembly is a reasonable bet here and a bad one elsewhere.

It means the robot is never taught a cabinet. The same parametric model that emits the cut list and the G‑code emits the assembly sequence — panel order, insertion vectors, clamp positions. Batch size one costs nothing, because the program is generated per box rather than selected from a library.

The case clamp is not the constraint — feeding it is

A through-feed case clamp closes and squares one cabinet a minute. At 14 boxes a kitchen that is 53 machine-hours a year at 228 kitchens, and 142 at 610. The clamp will never be busy. All the engineering is in getting the right five panels to it, the right way up, with the connectors already in — which is a part-handling and sequencing problem, not an assembly problem.

That is the distinction worth holding onto. We are not building a robot that understands cabinets. We are building a robot that places panels a parametric model has already told it exactly where to put.

Why this order and not all at once

Every step is triggered by throughput, not by ambition, and each pays for itself before the next is bought. 10

Autonomous coding

The stack in section 05 is perhaps 40,000 lines of software. Three years ago that was a team of six. It is now two people and a fleet of coding agents — for three specific reasons.

  1. The specification is already formal. Frameless cabinetry is the 32mm system: standardised hole spacing, standardised hardware, standardised panel logic. This is not a domain that has to be invented — it has to be encoded, and encoding a precise spec is exactly what coding agents are good at.
  2. The tests are mechanical. Does the generated box close? Do the panel dimensions sum to the carcass? Do the parts nest inside a 4×8 sheet? Does the hardware schedule match the door count? Every one of these is checkable by a program, which means agents can iterate against ground truth instead of against an opinion.
  3. The blast radius is bounded. Generated code produces a proposed cut list, and the cut list is simulated before it reaches a machine. A bad generation wastes a run, not a hand.

The part that is genuinely defensible

Anyone can buy the same router. Nobody else has a system that turns a customer holding a laser into a priced, engineered, machine-ready kitchen in under an hour. The pipeline from customer intent to machine code is the company. The factory is both its proof and its first market — see section 10 for why that pipeline is worth more than the cabinets it cuts.

What we are actually building

It is worth being explicit about the shape of this company, because the cabinets obscure it. We are a software company that owns a factory, presenting as a manufacturer. The factory is not the asset — it is the proof that the asset works, and the first market it is sold into.

The reason that matters is transferability. Strip the cabinet-specific parts away and what is left is a general pipeline: customer intent → parametric model → validated geometry → nested toolpaths → machine code → scheduled job → inspection. That pipeline does not know it is making kitchens.

The transferable asset is the cheap half

Six of the seven layers move to a different material with the library swapped and the post rewritten. The one that does not is the steel — and the steel is the $528,000. The $120,000 of software is what carries into the next market. That is an unusually good ratio, and it is the whole argument for building the software first and the factory second.

Sheet metal is the obvious second market: same nesting problem, same quote-to-code path, a fragmented supply base, and an existing set of companies proving customers will buy fabricated parts through a web configurator.

The market is already pricing this thesis

Hadrian Automation builds software-driven automated factories for aerospace and defence precision parts. Its Opus stack reads part designs and automates the machining and inspection around them — structurally the same idea as this plan, in a different material. In August 2026 it raised $1.37bn at a $7.87bn post-money valuation, on $2.34bn raised in total.

The relevant point is not the number, it is the multiple. Capital prices an automated factory as software, not as a machine shop — provided the autonomy is real and the stack is the thing being sold. Cabinets are a smaller, cheaper, faster proving ground for exactly that claim: $528k of steel against Hadrian's industrial scale, in a market where the customer is a homeowner rather than a defence prime.

Which does not change the build order

“Automate everything” is the right architecture and the wrong purchase schedule. Every interface should be designed for a machine to feed it and a machine to take from it, so nothing has to be rebuilt when the robots arrive. But buying them now would put automation on stations that already idle at 60% while the designer is saturated. Architect for lights-out; buy on demand. The distinction is the difference between this plan and a $4.78m plant with no customers.

Build order and return

Deliberately software first. The expensive, irreversible purchases come after the cheap, reversible proofs — and the return on each phase is wildly different, which decides the order on its own.

Phase 1 and 2 — no factory at all

For the first six months we do not own a machine. We build the software, sell kitchens designed by it, and subcontract every box to one of the 6,118 cabinet shops already in business. Phase 2 sends them machine-ready cut files rather than drawings, which is what proves the CAM half works before we buy anything to run it on.

Phase 3 is not close to anything else

Bringing manufacturing in-house nearly doubles contribution per kitchen, and at 228 kitchens that is $1.27m a year on $363,000 of machines — a three-month payback. Even at 100 kitchens it pays back inside eight months. Every hour spent on anything else before phase 3 is an hour spent on the wrong thing.

And the robots do not pay for themselves on labour

This is worth stating plainly rather than being found later. At 228 kitchens, phases 5, 6 and 7 have paybacks of ten, eleven and forty years. Labour is $32.85 an hour loaded, and there is simply not enough of it left in the plant after phase 4 for a robot to recover its own cost.

At 610 kitchens they improve to 3.7, 4.1 and 15 years — better, and still not the reason to build them. Phases 5 to 7 are not a cost-saving programme. They are R&D on the transferable asset, funded out of phase 3's margin. The justification is that the finished cell — a parametric model that emits geometry, toolpaths and assembly motion for a machine nobody touches — is the thing that carries into sheet metal. Judge them as product development, not as capex.

Why subcontract first

Phases 1 and 2 cost $120,000 and prove the entire commercial thesis — that customers will buy a kitchen designed by software — without owning a single machine. At a 35% subcontracted margin, break-even in that period is about 20 kitchens a year, under two a month. If it fails there, $408,000 is unspent. If it works, the router is bought against a real order book rather than a forecast.
